The Missing Ingredient in Most Churn Models

A SaaS company launches a retention intervention, personalized onboarding emails, a dedicated account manager, and a targeted discount offer. After six months, the data team reports something striking: among the 2,400 users who received the intervention, 12% churned. Among the 8,600 who did not, 23% churned. The VP of Data presents these numbers to the executive team and declares the intervention a clear success. The board approves a full rollout. Nobody asks the only question that matters: would those same users have churned at 12% even without the intervention?

They might have. The retention team did not assign the intervention at random; they targeted users who were already logging in regularly, exploring new features, and engaging with support. These users were less likely to churn regardless of whether anyone intervened. Prior engagement caused both selection into the intervention and lower churn. The 11-percentage-point gap is real, but it conflates the effect of the intervention with the effect of being the kind of user who gets selected for one. In causal language, engagement is a confounder: it sits on a backdoor path from intervention to churn, and failing to block that path makes the raw comparison uninformative about cause and effect.

This confusion is not unique to SaaS. Any organization that uses time-to-event outcomes, customer churn, employee turnover, loan default, equipment failure, faces the same trap when evaluating interventions. Standard descriptive tools tell you what happened to those who received a treatment. They do not tell you what would have happened had you assigned the treatment differently. The gap between these two questions is the subject of causal survival analysis.

Why Adjusting for Covariates Does Not Fix It

A well-trained analyst will object at this point and propose running a Cox proportional hazards regression, adjusting for login frequency, subscription tier, account age, and whatever else is measured. The adjusted hazard ratio will look more credible than the raw gap. But it does not solve the problem, for a reason that is deeper than confounding and that persists even in randomized trials.

The hazard ratio is not a clean causal estimand, even after adjustment, and even in the absence of unmeasured confounding (Hernán, 2010). There are two reasons for this, and the second is more fundamental than the first.

The first problem is that the hazard ratio can change over time. A Cox model typically reports a single number, a weighted average of time-specific ratios, and that average depends on how long the study runs. The same underlying reality could yield a hazard ratio of 1.8 at six months and something close to 1.0 at two years. A single summary statistic papers over a potentially meaningful time-varying effect.

The second problem is structural. The hazard at time \(t\) is defined as the instantaneous risk of churn among users who have not yet churned by \(t\). Surviving to \(t\) is itself affected by the intervention, which means the hazard ratio at \(t\) compares two groups, treated survivors and untreated survivors, that have been made incomparable by the act of conditioning on post-treatment survival. Over time, the treated group is selectively depleted of high-risk users who churned early, so the remaining treated users become systematically different from the remaining untreated users on unobserved susceptibility. A harmful intervention can appear protective at later time points simply because it has already eliminated the most vulnerable users from the risk set. The hazard ratio answers how the instantaneous risk compares between groups conditional on survival up to that moment, not how total survival time would change under a different treatment assignment. These are different questions, and the former does not generally imply the latter (Hernán, 2010). No amount of covariate adjustment within the Cox framework removes this built-in selection bias. The full formal argument is deferred to Chapter III, but the practical implication is immediate: if your effect measure can make a harmful intervention look protective, something is wrong with the measure.

The Counterfactual Question

What the VP of Data needed was not a better descriptive summary of two incomparable groups. She needed to answer a strictly counterfactual question: what would the churn rate have been had we assigned the intervention to every user, versus to no user? Not what happened to the users who received it, but what would have happened in a world where assignment was determined by policy rather than by engagement history.

Formally, let \(T^{(1)}\) denote the potential time-to-churn under universal treatment and \(T^{(0)}\) the potential time-to-churn under no treatment. The causal effect on the restricted mean survival time (RMST) over a horizon \(\tau\) is

\[\tau_{\text{RMST}} \;=\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[T^{(1)} \wedge \tau\right] - \mathbb{E}\!\left[T^{(0)} \wedge \tau\right] \;=\; \int_0^{\tau} \!\left(S^{(1)}(t) - S^{(0)}(t)\right) dt\]

where \(S^{(a)}(t) = P(T^{(a)} > t)\) is the counterfactual survival function under treatment arm \(a\), and \(a \wedge b = \min(a, b)\). This estimand is the area between two counterfactual survival curves. It requires no proportional-hazards assumption, carries no built-in selection bias, and answers the decision-maker’s question directly: how many additional months, on average, does the intervention keep a user subscribed over the window we care about?

The gap between the descriptive question (“who churned less?”) and the causal question (“what would happen if we intervened on everyone?”) is not academic. Business decisions are themselves counterfactuals: they concern what will happen when something is changed, not what already happened under the status quo. If a model does not answer a counterfactual question, it cannot justify a business decision. Without the tools of causal survival analysis, any claim about the intervention’s effect remains speculation dressed in statistics.

Chapter II. What Survival Analysis Actually Estimates

Why Standard Regression Fails

A natural first instinct when studying customer churn is to reach for ordinary least squares: regress time-to-churn on a set of covariates and read off the coefficients. Or perhaps discretize the problem, churned by month twelve versus not, and fit a logistic model. Both instincts are understandable, and both fail in systematic ways that are worth naming precisely.

The first problem is structural: survival times are almost always right-skewed, often heavily so, and OLS assumes roughly symmetric errors. Predictions can be negative, confidence intervals are distorted, and inference is inefficient. But the second problem is the decisive one. At any fixed analysis date, many subjects have not yet experienced the event. A customer who signed up three months ago and is still active contributes real information, her lifetime exceeds three months, but that information is incomplete. We do not know when she will churn. This is right-censoring, and OLS has no principled mechanism for handling it. Dropping censored observations introduces selection bias, since longer-tenured customers are precisely those most likely to be censored. Treating the censored time as the true event time biases every estimate downward. Neither option is acceptable.

Formally, for subject \(i\) we observe the pair \((\tilde{T}_i, \Delta_i)\), where

\[\tilde{T}_i = \min(T_i,\, C_i)\]

is the observed time, \(T_i\) is the latent true event time, \(C_i\) is the censoring time, and \(\Delta_i = \mathbf{1}(T_i \leq C_i)\) is the event indicator. The critical identifying assumption is non-informative censoring: \(T_i \perp C_i \mid \mathbf{X}_i\), meaning that the reason a subject is censored carries no information about when they would have experienced the event, conditional on covariates. Under this assumption, a censored observation at time \(\tilde{T}_i\) tells us unambiguously that \(T_i > \tilde{T}_i\), and this partial information can be exploited correctly. Survival analysis is the set of tools that does exactly this. This notation, \(\tilde{T}_i\) for the observed time and \(\Delta_i\) for the event indicator, is used throughout the rest of this post.

Logistic regression fares no better than OLS. It forces an arbitrary dichotomization of the time axis and discards the ordering information that makes time-to-event data so rich. A customer who churns on day five and one who churns on day two hundred and ninety are treated identically if both fall before the chosen cutoff. That is not a model of churn, it is a compression of churn into something that ignores its most important feature.

The Survival Function \(S(t)\) and the Hazard Function \(h(t)\)

Two complementary mathematical objects sit at the core of time-to-event analysis.

The survival function is defined as

\[S(t) = P(T > t), \quad t \geq 0.\]

It gives the probability that a subject has not yet experienced the event by time \(t\). For a SaaS platform, \(S(t)\) is the retention curve: the proportion of a customer cohort still active at month \(t\). It satisfies \(S(0) = 1\), is non-increasing, and in theory approaches zero as \(t \to \infty\), though in practice we observe it only down to some positive value determined by the length of follow-up. If \(T\) is continuous with density \(f(t)\) and CDF \(F(t)\), then \(S(t) = 1 - F(t) = \int_t^{\infty} f(u)\, du\). A value of \(S(12) = 0.68\) means that 68% of customers are still active twelve months after sign-up, a concrete, immediately interpretable retention figure.

The hazard function answers a different question: not how many customers remain, but at what rate are those who remain leaving right now? Formally,

\[h(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{P(t \leq T < t + \Delta t \mid T \geq t)}{\Delta t}.\]

This is an instantaneous conditional rate, not a probability. For a small interval \(\Delta t\), the quantity \(h(t)\,\Delta t\) approximates the probability that a customer who is still active at month \(t\) churns before month \(t + \Delta t\). A value of \(h(6) = 0.04\) means that among customers who have survived to month six, roughly 4% churn per month at that moment. Think of \(h(t)\) as the churn pressure currently being exerted on the surviving base: it can be high in the first weeks after onboarding (early friction), fall as loyal customers reveal themselves, and potentially rise again as contracts approach renewal dates.

The two functions are not independent. They are linked by the identity

\[S(t) = \exp\!\left(-\int_0^t h(u)\, du\right) = \exp(-H(t)),\]

where \(H(t) = \int_0^t h(u)\, du\) is the cumulative hazard. This relationship has a clean interpretation: the survival probability at time \(t\) equals the exponentiated negative sum of all instantaneous churn pressures accumulated from the start up to \(t\). Every month of elevated hazard compounds against the survival curve, and the curve is the running product of surviving those accumulated risks.

The Kaplan-Meier Estimator

Given data \((\tilde{T}_i, \Delta_i)\) for \(i = 1, \ldots, n\), the challenge is to estimate \(S(t)\) without imposing a parametric form on the event time distribution. The Kaplan-Meier estimator is the non-parametric maximum likelihood solution. Let \(t_{(1)} < t_{(2)} < \cdots < t_{(k)}\) be the ordered distinct event times observed in the sample, \(d_j\) the number of events at time \(t_{(j)}\), and \(n_j\) the number of subjects still at risk (under follow-up and event-free) just before \(t_{(j)}\). The estimator is

\[\hat{S}(t) = \prod_{j:\, t_{(j)} \leq t} \left(1 - \frac{d_j}{n_j}\right).\]

The logic is multiplicative: at each event time, the conditional probability of surviving that instant is \((n_j - d_j)/n_j\), and the survival probability at \(t\) is the product of all such conditional probabilities up to \(t\). Censored observations contribute to the risk set \(n_j\) for all times up to their censoring point and then exit, they never appear in the numerator \(d_j\) but they support the denominator for earlier intervals, which is exactly how their partial information enters the estimate (Hosmer, Lemeshow & May, 2008, Chapters 1-2). The result is a right-continuous step function that drops only at observed event times and remains flat between them.

For a customer cohort, the Kaplan-Meier curve is the empirical retention curve, and it is more honest than naive proportion-remaining calculations precisely because it accounts correctly for customers observed over different lengths of time. It is the natural descriptive baseline before any modelling begins.

Restricted Mean Survival Time

The Kaplan-Meier curve is visually informative but stakeholders routinely want a single summary number: how long, on average, does a customer stay? The naive mean survival time is undefined when the survival curve does not reach zero, which is the typical case when follow-up is finite and some subjects are censored, because the integral \(\int_0^\infty \hat{S}(t)\, dt\) diverges. The Restricted Mean Survival Time (RMST) resolves this by truncating the integral at a pre-specified horizon \(\tau\):

\[\text{RMST}(\tau) = \int_0^{\tau} S(t)\, dt.\]

Geometrically, \(\text{RMST}(\tau)\) is the area under the survival curve from zero to \(\tau\). If \(\tau = 24\) months and \(\text{RMST}(24) = 17.4\), the interpretation is direct: a customer is expected to remain active for 17.4 of the first 24 months after sign-up. That sentence can be delivered in a board presentation and immediately converted into expected revenue per customer.

Contrast this with the hazard ratio produced by a Cox proportional hazards model. A hazard ratio of 0.72 says that the treated group experiences a 28% reduction in the instantaneous churn rate relative to control. This is a relative measure on an abstract scale. It does not tell you whether the improvement translates into two additional days of customer lifetime or two additional years, that depends entirely on the baseline hazard, which the hazard ratio does not report. A decision-maker weighing the cost of an intervention against its benefit needs an absolute quantity in interpretable units. \(\Delta\,\text{RMST}(\tau) = 1.5\) months means the intervention extends expected active lifetime by 1.5 months within the two-year window. That figure, multiplied by average monthly revenue, is a CLV calculation. A hazard ratio cannot do that arithmetic directly.

A second advantage of RMST is robustness. The hazard ratio is only interpretable as a constant if the proportional hazards assumption holds, that the ratio \(h_1(t)/h_0(t)\) is the same at every time \(t\). This assumption fails routinely in practice, particularly when treatment effects evolve over time or when subgroups have crossing survival curves. The RMST requires no such assumption. It is defined and estimable under any survival distribution, which makes it an attractive target for causal inference, as we will develop in Chapter III.

NoteChoosing \(\tau\)

The truncation horizon \(\tau\) should be set to a time point well within the observed follow-up of all groups, so that \(\hat{S}(\tau)\) is reliably estimated. A common choice is the minimum of the largest observed event time across groups. Setting \(\tau\) too large inflates variance; setting it too small discards information. In business applications, \(\tau\) often corresponds naturally to a contract horizon, a fiscal year, or the length of a planned experiment.

What These Tools Cannot Do

Every quantity defined and estimated in this chapter, \(S(t)\), \(h(t)\), the Kaplan-Meier curve, and the RMST, is purely descriptive. These tools characterize the observed survival experience of a group under study. They do not adjust for confounding, and they do not identify causal effects. A Kaplan-Meier curve stratified by treatment group conflates the effect of the treatment with any pre-existing differences between the groups that influenced who received it. The log-rank test detects a difference between groups without attributing it to a cause. The Cox proportional hazards model conditions on covariates, but its coefficients remain associational unless a credible identification strategy underlies the analysis, a strategy the model itself does not supply.

To move from “customers who received the onboarding intervention stay longer” to “the intervention causes customers to stay longer,” we need the causal inference layer, and that layer must contend with the specific complications that time introduces: time-varying confounders, selection on survival, and the non-collapsibility of the hazard ratio. Chapter III builds that identification architecture, explaining why the log-rank test and the standard Cox model are insufficient for causal claims and introducing the estimands and assumptions that support valid causal survival analysis.

Chapter III. Why the Log-Rank Test Is Not Enough

Chapters I and II gave you the descriptive vocabulary of survival analysis: Kaplan-Meier curves, cumulative hazard functions, the machinery for handling censoring. That vocabulary is genuinely useful, but it is also quietly dangerous. It is possible to run a log-rank test, fit a Cox model, obtain a hazard ratio of 0.85, and walk into a leadership meeting with a number that looks like an answer. This chapter is about why that number is probably not the answer you think it is, and in some settings, not an answer at all.

The Log-Rank Test Cannot See Confounding

The log-rank test compares two observed Kaplan-Meier curves. Under the null hypothesis that the two survival functions are identical, it constructs a weighted sum of the differences between observed and expected event counts at each event time, and tests whether that sum is distinguishable from zero. The test is nonparametric and distribution-free, which makes it elegant. It is also, in the precise statistical sense, unadjusted.

What this means in practice is that the log-rank test compares groups as they appear in the data, not as they would appear in an ideal experiment. Suppose a subscription platform offers a discount to users who click a re-engagement banner. Those users are more engaged to begin with: they open emails, explore features, and were unlikely to churn regardless of the discount. The superior retention observed in the treated group is then a mixture of the campaign’s effect and the pre-existing advantages of whoever self-selected into it. The log-rank test has no mechanism for disentangling these contributions. It measures association between treatment status and survival, full stop. When treatment assignment is confounded by covariates that also affect the time-to-event outcome, the test statistic will be inflated, or deflated, by exactly that confounding, and there is no algebraic device inside the test that can correct for it.

The problem is not ignorance of confounding on the part of statisticians who use the log-rank test. The problem is that the test was designed to answer a descriptive question (do these two groups differ in survival experience?) and it is routinely recruited to answer a causal question (does the treatment change survival?). Those are not the same question, and conflating them is the foundational error of naive survival analysis.

The Cox Model as an Associational Tool

The natural reflex is to reach for the Cox proportional hazards model, which adjusts for observed confounders and so appears to solve the identification problem. The standard Cox model specifies the conditional hazard for individual \(i\) as

\[ h(t \mid X_i, A_i) = h_0(t) \exp(X_i^\top \beta + \gamma A_i), \]

where \(h_0(t)\) is an unspecified baseline hazard, \(X_i\) is a vector of observed confounders, and \(A_i \in \{0, 1\}\) is the treatment indicator. The coefficient \(\gamma\) yields an adjusted hazard ratio \(\exp(\gamma)\) that conditions on \(X\). This is better than the unadjusted log-rank comparison.

But “adjusted” is not the same as “causal,” and the distinction matters enormously. The hazard ratio that Cox produces is a conditional parameter: it compares the instantaneous risk of the event at time \(t\) for two individuals who share the same covariate values \(X\) but differ in treatment. The quantity of interest for causal inference is a marginal parameter: the effect of assigning the entire population to treatment versus control, averaging over the distribution of \(X\) in that population. The Cox model conditions on \(X\) but does not marginalize over it.

As Voinot et al. (2025) make explicit, the hazard ratio derived from a Cox model does not represent a causal contrast even in randomized trials, due to selection bias that accumulates in the risk sets over time. This is not a failure of implementation; it is a structural property of the hazard ratio as an estimand. There is additionally the problem of non-collapsibility: even under correct specification and no unmeasured confounding, the conditional hazard ratio from a Cox model will systematically differ from the marginal hazard ratio in the population, because the hazard ratio is a non-collapsible measure (Huitfeldt, Stensrud & Suzuki, 2019). The marginal causal effect cannot be recovered by averaging the conditional estimates across covariate strata. The adjusted Cox HR is not a stepping stone to the causal effect; it is a fundamentally different object.

The Proportional Hazards Assumption

Beneath both problems sits a third, which compounds them. The Cox model does not simply estimate a hazard ratio; it assumes that the hazard ratio is constant over time. Formally, the proportional hazards (PH) assumption states that

\[ \frac{h(t \mid A = 1, X)}{h(t \mid A = 0, X)} = \exp(\gamma) \quad \text{for all } t, \]

where the right-hand side is a constant independent of \(t\). If a treated user has a 15% lower instantaneous churn hazard than an untreated user at day 30, the PH assumption requires that same 15% reduction to hold at day 90, day 180, and day 365. The two groups’ hazard functions are parallel on the log scale for all time, forever.

That is a strong assumption. Grambsch & Therneau (1994) showed that non-proportionality can be expressed as a time-varying coefficient: if the true hazard is \(h(t \mid A_i) = h_0(t) \exp\{\beta(t) A_i\}\), then PH is simply the restriction \(\beta(t) = \gamma\) for all \(t\). Violations occur whenever \(\beta(t)\) trends, jumps, or decays, and they are far more common in business data than in the clinical trials for which Cox’s model was originally designed.

Think concretely about a retention intervention: an in-app notification sent to users on day 7 of a free trial. The effect of that nudge is almost certainly largest in the first two or three weeks, when it changes immediate behavior. By month 6, users who stayed have either converted or are deeply habituated; the notification from five months ago is no longer a meaningful driver of their current survival probability. The hazard ratio starts large in magnitude and decays toward null as time passes. Any model that assumes otherwise is not just slightly wrong; it is misspecified in a way that systematically distorts every downstream inference.

The Danger of a Single Summary Measure

When the PH assumption fails, the Cox model does not produce an error. It continues to produce a coefficient, a standard error, and a confidence interval. What it produces is a time-weighted average of a heterogeneous effect, and that average is deeply misleading.

This is the central argument of Hernán (2010). Using the Women’s Health Initiative randomized trial of hormone therapy, he shows that the reported average hazard ratio of 1.24 was composed of year-specific ratios of 1.81, 1.34, 1.27, 1.25, 1.45, and 0.70 for successive years of follow-up. The single summary of 1.24 would have been approximately 1.80 if the trial had been halted after year one, or close to 1.00 if followed long enough for the declining trend to average out. The 24% increase in coronary heart disease risk that made international headlines was, in part, an artifact of the arbitrary choice of follow-up duration, not a stable property of the treatment.

The business translation is direct. A VP of Data who sees \(\hat{\gamma} \rightarrow \exp(\hat{\gamma}) = 0.85\) will assume a constant 15% reduction in churn risk at every point in the customer lifecycle. If instead the intervention had a large effect in weeks one through three and essentially no effect thereafter, the “0.85” is an average of a time-varying \(\beta(t)\) that ranged from, say, 0.60 in week one to 0.98 by month six. Planning retention budget based on a constant effect that does not actually exist at scale is not a minor analytical error; it is a strategic misallocation built on a statistical artifact.

Detecting this problem requires testing. The canonical diagnostic is cox.zph() from the survival package, which implements the Grambsch & Therneau (1994) score test. The test regresses the scaled Schoenfeld residuals, defined at each event time \(t_k\) as \(r_k(\hat{\gamma}) = A_{(k)} - M(\hat{\gamma}, t_k)\), where \(A_{(k)}\) is the treatment indicator of the subject who experienced the event and \(M(\hat{\gamma}, t_k)\) is the risk-weighted mean treatment at \(t_k\), against a function of time. Under the PH assumption, the scaled residuals should have zero trend. A significant slope is evidence of nonproportionality. Plotting the smoothed scaled residuals against event times reveals the functional form of \(\beta(t)\): whether the treatment effect grows, decays, or reverses direction.

Two Failures, One Trap

Stepping back, the standard survival toolkit presents two distinct but compounding failures for causal inference. The first is confounding: when treatment is not randomly assigned, the log-rank test and the Cox model capture selection into treatment, not the effect of treatment. Adding covariates to the Cox model reduces this problem but does not solve it, because the conditional hazard ratio is not the same quantity as the marginal causal effect, and because unmeasured confounders are simply absent from the model. The second failure is temporal heterogeneity: even when confounding is absent, the PH assumption licenses the compression of a potentially rich, time-varying effect into a single number that may accurately describe no particular moment in the follow-up period.

What is conspicuously absent from the standard toolkit is any formal language for counterfactual reasoning. The log-rank test and the Cox model describe what happened to groups we observed. They have no native vocabulary for asking what would have happened to a unit under a treatment it did not receive, the foundational question of causal inference. Standard survival analysis describes what was observed, not what would have happened under a different policy.

Those two questions have different answers, and conflating them is why a single hazard ratio from a business survival analysis can be simultaneously precise and causally meaningless.

Standard survival analysis lacks the vocabulary to answer causal questions about censored time-to-event outcomes. Chapter IV builds that vocabulary from the ground up, introducing the potential outcomes framework adapted for survival data, defining counterfactual event times \(T^{(1)}\) and \(T^{(0)}\), stating the identifying assumptions that connect them to what we observe, and motivating the restricted mean survival time as a causal estimand that remains interpretable even when proportional hazards fails.

Chapter IV. Potential Outcomes Meet Censored Data

Causal inference requires comparing two worlds that cannot both be observed. Survival analysis compounds this problem by adding a second source of missingness: the event may not have occurred yet when we stop watching. Before we can estimate anything useful, we need to formalize exactly what we are trying to learn and what we are willing to assume.

Potential Outcomes for Survival Times

Following the Rubin Causal Model (Rubin, 1974), attach to each individual \(i\) two potential event times:

  • \(T_i^{(1)}\): the time at which individual \(i\) would experience the event if assigned to treatment (\(A_i = 1\)).
  • \(T_i^{(0)}\): the time at which individual \(i\) would experience the event if assigned to control (\(A_i = 0\)).

These are not sequential observations. They are parallel counterfactual quantities, only one of which can ever be realized. This is the fundamental problem of causal inference applied to survival data (Hernán & Robins, 2020, Chapter 17). We observe:

\[T_i = A_i \cdot T_i^{(1)} + (1 - A_i) \cdot T_i^{(0)}\]

where \(A_i \in \{0,1\}\) is the treatment indicator. For every individual, one potential outcome is structurally missing. A treated customer never reveals how long they would have retained under the control condition. A control customer never reveals how quickly they would have churned had they received the onboarding intervention.

The situation is further complicated by censoring. Even the one potential outcome we do observe may be cut off. As established in Chapter II, we do not observe \(T_i\) directly. We observe \(\tilde{T}_i = \min(T_i, C_i)\) and the event indicator \(\Delta_i = \mathbf{1}\{T_i \leq C_i\}\), where \(C_i\) is the censoring time (Voinot et al., 2025, Section 1.1). A customer who cancels at month 14 gives us a clean signal. A customer who is still active at the end of the observation window gives us only the information that their event time exceeds that window. This is the doubly missing structure that defines causal survival analysis: the counterfactual we never observe can additionally be right-censored, so we know neither which world they belong to nor how long they survived in it.

SUTVA in the Time-to-Event Setting

Identification requires the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (Rubin, 1974; Voinot et al., 2025, Assumption 1.1). SUTVA has two components that are equally relevant for time-to-event outcomes.

No interference. The potential outcome for individual \(i\) depends only on \(i\)’s own treatment, not on the treatment assigned to any other unit. In a SaaS churn study, one customer’s exposure to the new onboarding flow does not affect another customer’s churn decision. Interference would arise if the treatment unlocked a collaborative feature requiring a network of treated peers; in that case, \(T_i^{(1)}\) would depend on how many neighbors were also treated, violating the assumption.

Consistency. The observed event time equals the potential event time under the treatment actually received: \(T_i = T_i^{(A_i)}\). This rules out hidden versions of treatment. If “treatment” is a premium tier discount but the discount magnitude varies uncontrolled across users, then \(T_i^{(1)}\) is not a single well-defined quantity, and the consistency link breaks.

Together, these conditions are compactly encoded as:

\[T_i = A_i \cdot T_i^{(1)} + (1 - A_i) \cdot T_i^{(0)}\]

The Causal Estimand: RMST Difference

We need a single number that summarizes the causal effect of treatment on survival. The natural candidate, the hazard ratio, is the wrong choice for causal work, for reasons that are both statistical and conceptual, and that were established formally in Chapter III.

The hazard at time \(t\) conditions on surviving up to \(t\), a post-treatment variable. Even in a randomized trial, conditioning on this collider opens a backdoor path between treatment and unmeasured risk factors and induces spurious associations (Hernán & Robins, 2020, Fine Point 17.2), the same selection-on-survival mechanism introduced narratively in Chapter I and proven formally in Chapter III. The hazard ratio is also non-collapsible, as established in Chapter III: a marginal hazard ratio cannot be recovered by marginalizing over a conditional one, so heterogeneous populations produce hazard ratios that cannot be meaningfully aggregated (Voinot et al., 2025, Section 1). And under non-proportional hazards, the default in business contexts where treatment effects decay or accumulate over time, the Cox model estimates a weighted average of time-specific ratios with weights that depend on the censoring distribution, producing a quantity that shifts from study to study even when the underlying customer behavior is identical (Uno et al., 2014).

The preferred estimand is the difference in restricted mean survival time (RMST). Define the counterfactual survival functions:

\[S^{(a)}(t) = P\!\left(T_i^{(a)} > t\right), \quad a \in \{0, 1\}\]

Because \(T_i^{(a)} \wedge \tau\) is a non-negative random variable, its expectation equals the integral of its survival function (Voinot et al., 2025, Equation 1):

\[E\!\left[T_i^{(a)} \wedge \tau\right] = \int_0^\tau S^{(a)}(t)\, dt\]

The causal estimand is the RMST difference:

\[\tau_{\text{RMST}} = E\!\left[T_i^{(1)} \wedge \tau - T_i^{(0)} \wedge \tau\right] = \int_0^\tau \!\left[S^{(1)}(t) - S^{(0)}(t)\right] dt\]

This is the area between the two counterfactual survival curves from \(0\) to \(\tau\), the same quantity introduced as a teaser in Chapter I and now derived from first principles. The interpretation requires no modelling assumptions to communicate: if \(\tau_{\text{RMST}} = 2.3\) months with \(\tau = 24\) months, the typical treated customer enjoys 2.3 more active months in the first two years than they would have under control. The VP of Data can price that in revenue. The hazard ratio 0.87 requires explaining what a hazard is, why it is not constant, and why the reported figure is a reweighted average of something that was never constant to begin with.

Uno et al. (2014) formalized this argument in a clinical context and the logic transfers directly to product analytics. RMST is collapsible, marginal, and has a direct business interpretation, it is, literally, extra months of customer lifetime gained. The choice of \(\tau\) is a design decision, not a statistical one: it should reflect the time horizon over which retention gains are commercially meaningful.

Identification with Censoring

We have defined the target, \(\tau_{\text{RMST}}\), in terms of counterfactual survival curves that are never fully observed. To identify the estimand from observed data, we need assumptions that connect the potential outcomes to the censored variables. Two distinct problems must be addressed: the missing counterfactuals problem and the censoring problem.

Unconfoundedness (conditional exchangeability). In observational settings:

\[\{T_i^{(0)},\, T_i^{(1)}\} \perp\!\!\!\perp A_i \mid X_i\]

Conditional on measured covariates \(X_i\), treatment assignment is independent of the potential event times. In a SaaS churn context, \(X_i\) might include plan tier, usage intensity, firmographics, and prior support interactions. Any factor that simultaneously predicts both treatment receipt and churn, and is absent from \(X\), violates this assumption and confounds the estimate.

Positivity of treatment. \(0 < P(A_i = 1 \mid X_i = x) < 1\) for all \(x\) in the support of \(X\). Every customer profile must have a positive probability of appearing in both treatment groups.

Conditional independence of censoring (Voinot et al., 2025, Assumption 2.5). Right-censoring is a missing data problem: the event time is unobserved above \(C_i\). For identification:

\[\{T_i^{(0)},\, T_i^{(1)}\} \perp\!\!\!\perp C_i \mid X_i,\, A_i\]

Within strata defined by covariates and treatment, subjects censored at time \(t\) are representative of all subjects still at risk at time \(t\). Identification fails when unmeasured factors drive both the event and the censoring. In a SaaS context, the clearest violation is competitive displacement: a customer evaluating a competitor’s product will simultaneously reduce platform engagement and become harder to track, they may stop logging in before formally cancelling. Their reduced engagement (unmeasured) predicts both earlier churn and earlier effective censoring. Conditional on observed \(X\), their censoring time is no longer independent of their potential event time, and naive survival estimators yield biased causal estimates.

Positivity of the censoring process (Voinot et al., 2025, Assumption 2.4). There exists \(\varepsilon > 0\) such that \(G(\tau \mid A, X) \geq \varepsilon\) almost surely, where \(G(t) = P(C \geq t)\). Every subject must have a positive probability of remaining uncensored through the analysis horizon. If entire covariate strata are systematically censored before \(\tau\), the survival function is simply not identified in those strata.

One scope limitation is worth stating plainly. The conditional independence assumption above is written in terms of baseline covariates \(X_i\), fixed at the start of follow-up. It does not cover settings where censoring is additionally driven by covariates that evolve after treatment begins, a customer downgrading their plan mid-contract before eventually cancelling, say. Handling that properly requires restating the assumption over the full time-varying covariate history and estimating with time-varying weights, the marginal structural model territory of Robins, Hernán & Brumback (2000). Everything in this post, including the IPCW construction in Chapter VI, assumes censoring is adequately described by the baseline covariates already in \(X_i\).

Under SUTVA, unconfoundedness, positivity of treatment, conditional independence of censoring, and censoring positivity, the identification chain proceeds as follows (Voinot et al., 2025, Equation 16):

\[\tau_{\text{RMST}} = E\!\left[T(1) \wedge \tau - T(0) \wedge \tau\right] = E\!\left[E\!\left[T \wedge \tau \mid X, A=1\right] - E\!\left[T \wedge \tau \mid X, A=0\right]\right]\]

Each conditional expectation on the right-hand side involves only observable quantities: \(\tilde{T}\), \(\Delta\), \(A\), and \(X\). The missing counterfactuals have been replaced by conditional regressions over the observed data. We have, in principle, a recipe for identification. The estimation problem remains.

Chapter V. G-Computation for Survival Outcomes

From Potential Outcomes to Practical Estimation

In Chapter IV, we established the potential outcomes framework for survival data. We defined the counterfactual survival curves \(S^{(1)}(t)\) and \(S^{(0)}(t)\), identified the conditions under which they are estimable from observational data, and recognized that the central challenge is confounding: treated and control individuals differ systematically in their baseline characteristics, so naively comparing their Kaplan-Meier curves does not recover a causal effect. We now face the operational question: how do we actually estimate these curves from data? The first and most natural strategy is g-computation, also known as the g-formula or direct standardization, introduced by Robins (1986) in the context of mortality studies with sustained exposures. The idea is elegantly simple: fit a model for the outcome given treatment and confounders, then use that model to simulate “what if” scenarios for the entire population under each treatment level.

The “What If” Simulation

Suppose we have observed data on \(n\) individuals, each with a survival time \(T_i\) (possibly censored), a treatment indicator \(A_i \in \{0, 1\}\), and a vector of baseline confounders \(\mathbf{X}_i\). The g-computation algorithm proceeds in three steps.

First, we fit a survival model for \(T\) given \(A\) and \(\mathbf{X}\) on the observed data. A Cox proportional hazards model is the standard choice, capturing the conditional hazard as \(\lambda(t \mid A, \mathbf{X})\). Second, we artificially set \(A = 1\) for every individual in the dataset, regardless of their actual treatment assignment, and use the fitted model to predict each person’s survival curve \(\hat{S}(t \mid A = 1, \mathbf{X} = \mathbf{x}_i)\). We average these individual predictions across the entire sample to obtain the marginal counterfactual curve:

\[\hat{S}^{(1)}(t) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \hat{S}(t \mid A = 1, \mathbf{X} = \mathbf{x}_i)\]

Third, we repeat the procedure with \(A = 0\) forced on everyone, yielding \(\hat{S}^{(0)}(t)\). These two curves are the g-computation estimates of the causal survival functions under treatment and control, respectively.

The averaging step is the standardization: by marginalizing over the empirical covariate distribution, we recover the population-level counterfactual quantities that were the target of Chapter IV. Intuitively, we are asking: “If everyone in our sample, with all their observed heterogeneity in tenure, usage, and baseline risk, had received the treatment, what would the average survival curve look like?”, and then repeating the question for control. This is the statistical equivalent of running a randomized trial in silico, using the rich covariate data we already have to simulate balance across both arms.

A natural question at this point is what happened to censoring, given the weight Chapter IV placed on conditional independence and positivity of the censoring process. The Cox model already accounts for censored observations through its risk-set construction (each subject contributes to the risk set only while under observation and free of the event, exactly the mechanism described for the Kaplan-Meier estimator in Chapter II), so g-computation inherits whatever protection against non-informative censoring the outcome model provides. It does not, however, do anything extra to correct for informative censoring. That problem, and the inverse-probability-of-censoring weighting needed to address it directly, is the subject of Chapter VI.

Why This Solves the Confounding Problem

Confounding arises because the distribution of \(\mathbf{X}\) differs between the treated and control groups in observational data. A naive comparison of Kaplan-Meier curves by treatment arm conflates the effect of treatment with the effect of the confounders associated with treatment assignment. G-computation breaks this association by construction. When we predict outcomes under \(A = 1\) for every individual, we are constructing a hypothetical world in which treatment is assigned universally and is therefore statistically independent of \(\mathbf{X}\). The same holds when we predict under \(A = 0\). By comparing the two hypothetical worlds rather than the two observed subpopulations, we isolate the causal effect of treatment from the confounding structure, provided the outcome model is right; the next section returns to what happens when it is not. This is the standardization principle applied to the causal inference setting: the marginal curves are weighted averages of the conditional curves, where the weights reflect the full population distribution of confounders rather than the treatment-conditional distributions.

The practical implication for decision-makers is immediate. Instead of reporting a hazard ratio of 0.8, which is difficult to interpret and prone to misunderstanding, you can now say: “If we roll out this intervention to all customers, we expect their average active lifetime to increase by 2.3 months, adjusted for their initial engagement levels.” G-computation converts an opaque relative measure into an actionable absolute one, grounded in the actual covariate distribution of the target population.

The adjustedCurves Package

Implementing g-computation for survival outcomes from scratch is deceptively complex. One must correctly extract baseline hazard estimates from the Cox model, handle tied event times, manage censoring in the counterfactual predictions, and construct valid confidence intervals that propagate uncertainty through the entire standardization procedure. The adjustedCurves package (Denz, 2024) provides a unified, battle-tested interface that abstracts away these complexities.

The package wraps g-computation, IPTW, AIPTW, matching, and pseudo-value approaches behind a single consistent function call. For g-computation specifically, it handles Cox model predictions correctly, applies standardization over the observed covariate distribution, and constructs confidence intervals via nonparametric bootstrapping, the recommended approach for variance estimation in this setting, given the complexity of analytical standard error formulas (Voinot et al., 2025, Section 3). Unlike older manual workflows that required piecing together multiple packages and custom bootstrap loops, adjustedCurves ensures that estimated survival curves satisfy the monotonicity and boundedness constraints automatically guaranteed by using a Cox outcome model.

The package was validated in the simulation study of Denz, Klaaßen-Mielke, and Timmesfeld (2023), which compared ten adjustment methods across a range of sample sizes and model specification scenarios. G-computation was the most efficient estimator (lowest standard error) when the outcome model was correctly specified, particularly when the confounders were strong predictors of survival. It outperformed both IPTW-based approaches and propensity score matching on variance grounds. The same study confirmed, however, that g-computation is not doubly robust: if the Cox model is misspecified, the counterfactual predictions are systematically biased, and no amount of bootstrapping can rescue them.

Estimating the Causal Effect: Adjusted RMST

Once we have the adjusted survival curves \(\hat{S}^{(1)}(t)\) and \(\hat{S}^{(0)}(t)\), summarizing the causal effect uses the same RMST machinery derived in Chapter IV. The restricted mean survival time up to a pre-specified time horizon \(\tau\) is the area under the counterfactual survival curve, \(\text{RMST}^{(a)}(\tau) = \int_0^{\tau} S^{(a)}(t) \, dt\), and the causal RMST difference is the g-computation plug-in estimate of \(\tau_{\text{RMST}}\):

\[\hat\tau_{\text{RMST}}(\tau) = \widehat{\text{RMST}}^{(1)}(\tau) - \widehat{\text{RMST}}^{(0)}(\tau) = \int_0^{\tau} \bigl[ \hat{S}^{(1)}(t) - \hat{S}^{(0)}(t) \bigr] \, dt\]

This quantity has a direct interpretation: it is the expected additional survival time up to horizon \(\tau\) that the population would accrue under treatment versus control. The horizon \(\tau\) should be chosen on business or clinical grounds, not by data-driven optimization, exactly as discussed in the callout box in Chapter II. In a customer retention context, for instance, \(\tau = 12\) months might correspond to the annual planning cycle, and \(\hat\tau_{\text{RMST}}(12)\) would quantify the expected gain in active customer-months from the intervention. The adjustedCurves package computes this automatically from the estimated curves via adjusted_rmst(), with bootstrap confidence intervals that properly propagate uncertainty from the outcome model and the censoring process alike.

# units, so the observed support is bounded and known in advance. This
# is what makes a fixed tau safe to choose below, rather than guessing
# at a horizon that might exceed whatever time happens to come out of
# the random draw above.

# not a bare 0/1 integer, both for coxph() and for adjustedsurv().
data$treatment <- factor(data$treatment, levels = c(0, 1))

# -----------------------------------------------------------------
# Step 1: Fit a Cox proportional hazards model on the observed data.
# Include treatment AND all confounders as predictors.
# x = TRUE is required by adjustedCurves for individual-level
# predictions at the standardization step.
# -----------------------------------------------------------------
cox_fit <- coxph(
  Surv(time, status) ~ treatment + covariate1 + covariate2,
  data = data,
  x    = TRUE
)

# method = "direct" invokes the g-formula (direct standardization):
#   the function sets treatment to each level for ALL individuals,
#   predicts individual survival curves, and averages them.
# conf_int = TRUE + bootstrap = TRUE: nonparametric bootstrap CIs
#   (n_boot = 500 is a reasonable default; use >= 1000 for
#   publication-quality estimates).
# -----------------------------------------------------------------
adj_curves <- adjustedsurv(
  data          = data,
  variable      = "treatment",
  ev_time       = "time",
  event         = "status",
  method        = "direct",
  outcome_model = cox_fit,
  conf_int      = TRUE,
  bootstrap     = TRUE,
  n_boot        = 10
)

# Step 3: Plot S^1(t) and S^0(t) with pointwise 95% CIs.

The Achilles Heel: Model Dependence

G-computation is only as good as the outcome model that underlies it. The estimator is consistent, under correct specification, and highly efficient, but this efficiency comes at a price. If the Cox model is misspecified, perhaps because a nonlinear covariate effect is modeled as linear, or because an important interaction is omitted, then the predictions \(\hat{S}(t \mid A = z, \mathbf{X}_i)\) are systematically wrong. When we average biased predictions over the covariate distribution, we obtain biased counterfactual curves. There is no self-correcting mechanism: g-computation places all its assumptions in the outcome model, and a wrong model produces a wrong answer regardless of sample size.

Denz et al. (2023) confirm this empirically: in scenarios with misspecified outcome models, the g-formula can exhibit persistent bias that does not shrink as \(n\) grows. Voinot et al. (2025) further show that the choice of modelling strategy matters, with single pooled models (“S-learners”) being particularly sensitive to proportional hazards violations compared to arm-specific models (“T-learners”). In practice, before committing to g-computation, you should check the proportional hazards assumption, assess whether functional forms are appropriate, and include all strong predictors of the outcome, not just the confounders, since omitting a prognostic variable can induce residual confounding through unobserved heterogeneity even after standardization.

G-computation gives us a powerful and efficient first tool. In well-specified settings, it outperforms every alternative reviewed in Denz et al. (2023) on variance grounds. But its reliance on a single correctly specified outcome model is a genuine vulnerability. Chapter VI confronts this directly: we turn to inverse probability weighting (IPW), which places its identifying assumptions in a treatment assignment model rather than an outcome model, and then to augmented inverse probability weighting (AIPW), which combines both model types to achieve double robustness, remaining consistent even if one of the two models is wrong.

Chapter VI. IPW and AIPW: When the Outcome Model Is Wrong

G-computation, as we saw in Chapter V, is elegant: fit an outcome model, predict counterfactual survival under each treatment, and average over the covariate distribution. But elegance is not robustness. If the Cox model is misspecified, a critical interaction omitted, a non-linearity ignored, the proportional hazards assumption silently violated, the counterfactual predictions are systematically biased, and no amount of additional data corrects the error. That bias is structural, not statistical: it does not shrink as the sample grows. If your data science team builds a flawed Cox model, g-computation will give the CEO the wrong answer, and not a small or self-correcting kind of wrong.

This chapter introduces two methods that escape that trap: inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) and its augmented extension, AIPW. We will see that survival data introduces a complication absent from the cross-sectional literature, informative censoring forces us to construct two sets of weights, not one, and that AIPW resolves nearly all of these concerns by combining the strengths of both approaches into a single, doubly robust estimator.

Inverse Probability of Treatment Weighting

IPTW takes a fundamentally different approach from g-computation. Instead of modelling the outcome, it models the treatment assignment mechanism. The intuition is geometric: in an observational study, treated and untreated customers differ systematically on their covariates. IPTW removes this imbalance by reweighting each observation by the inverse of its probability of receiving the treatment it actually received, constructing a pseudo-population where treatment assignment is independent of confounders (Robins, Hernán & Brumback, 2000).

Formally, let \(e(X_i) = P(A_i = 1 \mid X_i)\) denote the propensity score, estimated via logistic regression or more flexible machine learning methods. The IPTW weight for individual \(i\) is:

\[w_i^{\text{IPTW}} = \frac{A_i}{e(X_i)} + \frac{1 - A_i}{1 - e(X_i)}.\]

In the pseudo-population induced by these weights, the distribution of covariates is balanced across treatment arms, and a simple weighted comparison of survival curves recovers the marginal counterfactual survival curves \(S^{(a)}(t)\). Applied to survival data, the weights enter a weighted Kaplan-Meier estimator:

\[\hat{S}_{\text{IPTW}}(t \mid A = a) = \prod_{t_k \leq t} \left(1 - \frac{\sum_i w_i \cdot \mathbf{1}(\tilde{T}_i = t_k,\, \Delta_i = 1,\, A_i = a)}{\sum_i w_i \cdot \mathbf{1}(\tilde{T}_i \geq t_k,\, A_i = a)}\right),\]

first formalized by Cole & Hernán (2004) and Xie & Liu (2005). No outcome model is needed. This is the logic underlying marginal structural models (Robins, Hernán & Brumback, 2000), which provide a principled framework for causal inference whenever the treatment mechanism can be modelled, including, importantly, settings with time-varying confounding where standard regression adjustment fails entirely.

The Survival Twist: Inverse Probability of Censoring Weighting

Here is where survival analysis diverges from the cross-sectional IPW literature, and where practitioners most commonly go wrong. In addition to the selection bias introduced by non-random treatment assignment, survival data suffer from a second form of selection bias: informative censoring.

Consider a subscription business measuring time to customer churn. Highly engaged users are both less likely to churn (the event) and less likely to be lost to follow-up (censored). The observed, uncensored sample therefore overrepresents engaged users at later time points, making survival look artificially good. Standard IPTW corrects for treatment confounding but leaves this censoring bias entirely intact. Voinot et al. (2025) document exactly this pattern in their simulation study: unadjusted estimators exhibit substantial, persistent bias whenever the censoring mechanism depends on covariates.

The solution is inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW). Let \(G(t \mid X_i, A_i) = P(C_i \geq t \mid X_i, A_i)\) denote the conditional survival function of the censoring time. The IPCW transformation replaces each observed time with a reweighted version:

\[T_i^{\text{IPCW}} = \frac{\mathbf{1}(\Delta_i = 1) \cdot (\tilde{T}_i \wedge \tau)}{G(\tilde{T}_i \wedge \tau \mid X_i, A_i)},\]

upweighting uncensored individuals who were at high risk of censoring, so they represent the censored subjects they resemble. Individuals who are censored receive weight zero at all times after censoring.

In observational survival analysis under informative censoring, both weight sets are required simultaneously. The combined weight for individual \(i\) at time \(t\) is:

\[W_i(t) = \frac{1}{P(A_i \mid X_i)} \times \frac{1}{G(t \mid X_i, A_i)}.\]

The first factor balances confounding; the second corrects for informative censoring. This product creates a pseudo-population balanced on both the treatment assignment and censoring mechanisms. Both must be correctly specified for the IPTW-IPCW estimator to be consistent, which brings us directly to AIPW.

A Practical Warning: Stabilized Weights

Before augmentation, a necessary digression on weight pathology. Raw IPW weights can be extreme. A customer with \(e(X_i) = 0.02\) who happens to receive treatment gets a weight of 50. A handful of such observations dominate the weighted estimate, inflating variance to unacceptable levels and rendering confidence intervals almost meaningless. Denz et al. (2023) demonstrate this empirically: IPTW methods consistently show higher variance than g-computation, with the gap most pronounced in small samples and when propensity scores concentrate near the boundary.

The standard remedy is stabilized weights (Robins, Hernán & Brumback, 2000). Replace the numerator 1 with the marginal probability of the observed treatment:

\[sw_i = \frac{P(A_i = a_i)}{P(A_i = a_i \mid X_i = x_i)}.\]

Stabilized weights cluster around 1 rather than exhibiting the long right tail of unstabilized weights, yielding the same consistent estimates with substantially lower variance. The same stabilization applies to the censoring weights, replacing 1 with \(P(C_i > t)\) in the numerator. Always use stabilized weights in practice; the efficiency gain is essentially free.

Augmented IPW and Double Robustness

IPTW resolves g-computation’s single-model dependence, but creates a symmetric vulnerability: all causal conclusions now flow through the propensity and censoring models. Misspecify those, and the estimator fails. We have traded one fragility for another.

Augmented inverse probability weighting (AIPW), referred to as AIPTW in the survival literature, resolves this by combining both modelling strategies into a single estimating equation. The construction, rooted in the semiparametric efficiency theory of Scharfstein, Rotnitzky & Robins (1999) and implemented for adjusted survival curves by Denz et al. (2023), augments the IPTW estimator with a correction term derived from the outcome model:

\[\hat{S}_z^{\text{AIPW}}(t) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left[ W_i(t) \cdot \mathbf{1}(\tilde{T}_i > t) + \bigl(1 - W_i(t)\bigr) \cdot \hat{S}(t \mid A_i = z, X_i) \right].\]

The term \(\hat{S}(t \mid A_i = z, X_i)\) is the Cox model prediction, the same g-computation plug-in from Chapter V. When the weighting models are wrong, the outcome model term corrects the bias. When the outcome model is wrong, the weighting term corrects the bias. In simplified terms: AIPW is the IPTW estimator with g-computation predictions added back in as an augmentation, and each component acts as insurance for the other.

The formal property this construction delivers is double robustness, defined precisely as follows. The AIPW estimator \(\hat{S}_z^{\text{AIPW}}(t)\) is consistent for the true counterfactual survival curve \(S_z(t)\) if either:

  • the outcome model \(E[Y \mid A, X]\), equivalently, the conditional survival function \(S(t \mid A, X)\), is correctly specified, or
  • the propensity score model \(P(A \mid X)\) and the censoring survival model \(P(C \mid X, A)\) are both correctly specified,

even if the other set of models is misspecified. This is the guarantee Scharfstein, Rotnitzky & Robins (1999) established in the semiparametric efficiency framework: when both sets of models are correct, the AIPW estimator achieves the semiparametric efficiency bound, making it the most efficient estimator in its class. When only one is correct, it remains consistent, a property no single-model method can claim.

The practical implications are direct. If your data science team correctly models who receives treatment and which customers are censored but the Cox outcome model is misspecified, the AIPW estimate is still valid. Conversely, if the propensity and censoring models are wrong but the Cox model captures the true hazard, AIPW remains unbiased. The simulation evidence confirms this: across all five misspecification scenarios in Denz et al. (2023), AIPTW-based estimators remain unbiased whenever at least one model component is correct, while g-computation fails systematically the moment the outcome model is wrong. Voinot et al. (2025) extend this finding under informative censoring, showing that AIPW achieves the lowest mean squared error across a wide range of realistic scenarios, outperforming IPTW in finite samples, particularly when propensity scores are weak or censoring rates are high.

R Code: Adjusted Curves with IPW and AIPW

The adjustedCurves package (Denz et al., 2023) implements both methods through a unified interface. The central function is adjustedsurv(), dispatching to the appropriate estimator via the method argument. For IPTW, only the treatment model is required. For AIPW, all three models, outcome, treatment, and censoring, must be supplied; double robustness holds as long as the outcome model or the pair of propensity and censoring models is correctly specified.

Code 
library(survival)
library(adjustedCurves)

# ── 0. Generate sim_data ──────────────────────────────────────────────────
# A synthetic dataset with informative censoring: the censoring hazard
# depends on the same covariates that drive the event hazard, exactly the
# scenario this chapter argues standard IPTW alone cannot fix. treatment
# is stored as a factor, since adjustedsurv() requires a factor grouping
# variable (see the editor's note on this requirement in Chapter V).
set.seed(123)
n <- 1000

age       <- rnorm(n, mean = 50, sd = 12)
severity  <- rnorm(n, mean = 0, sd = 1)
biomarker <- rbinom(n, 1, 0.4)
treatment <- rbinom(n, 1, 0.5)

# Event time: hazard increases with age, severity, and biomarker presence;
# treatment reduces the hazard.
event_time <- rexp(
  n,
  rate = 0.04 + 0.10 * (severity > 0) + 0.05 * biomarker +
         0.002 * age - 0.02 * treatment
)

# Censoring time: deliberately informative, engaged (low-severity) patients
# are less likely to be censored early, mirroring the competitive-
# displacement mechanism described in Chapter IV.
censor_time <- rexp(n, rate = 0.03 + 0.04 * (severity > 0))

time  <- pmin(event_time, censor_time)
event <- as.integer(event_time <= censor_time)

sim_data <- data.frame(
  time      = time,
  event     = event,
  treatment = factor(treatment, levels = c(0, 1)),
  age       = age,
  severity  = severity,
  biomarker = biomarker
)

# ── 1. Fit the propensity score model ─────────────────────────────────────────
# Models P(A = 1 | X): the treatment assignment mechanism.
# One ingredient of double robustness; logistic regression is standard.
ps_model <- glm(
  treatment ~ age + severity + biomarker,
  data   = sim_data,
  family = binomial
)

# ── 2. Fit the outcome model (Cox) ────────────────────────────────────────────
# Models S(t | A, X): the conditional survival function.
# Required for AIPW. Misspecification here is protected against
# when the propensity + censoring models are correct.
cox_model <- coxph(
  Surv(time, event) ~ treatment + age + severity + biomarker,
  data = sim_data,
  x    = TRUE          # retain design matrix for prediction
)

# ── 3. Fit the censoring model ────────────────────────────────────────────────
# Models P(C > t | X, A): survival function of the censoring time.
# Needed whenever censoring depends on covariates (informative censoring).
# The event indicator is flipped: censoring is now the "event".
cens_model <- coxph(
  Surv(time, event == 0) ~ age + severity + biomarker,
  data = sim_data,
  x    = TRUE
)

# ── 4. IPTW-adjusted survival curves ──────────────────────────────────────────
# method = "iptw_km" (the IPTW Kaplan-Meier estimator of Xie & Liu (2005))
# weights observations by the inverse probability of the treatment they
# actually received, using the propensity score model only. No outcome
# model is required.
#
# A note on censoring: the adjustedCurves package does not let "iptw_km"
# take a separate censoring_model argument the way "aiptw" does below.
# Per the package documentation, the only way to fold in inverse
# probability of censoring weights here would be to multiply them into a
# single combined weight vector and pass that vector directly to
# treatment_model. This estimator therefore corrects for treatment
# confounding only, not for informative censoring, which is precisely
# why it is not the estimator of choice once dropout is suspected to
# depend on covariates, and precisely the gap AIPW closes next.
adj_ipw <- adjustedsurv(
  data            = sim_data,
  variable        = "treatment",
  ev_time         = "time",
  event           = "event",
  method          = "iptw_km",
  conf_int        = TRUE,
  treatment_model = ps_model       # P(A | X)
)

# ── 5. AIPW-adjusted survival curves (doubly robust) ─────────────────────────
# method = "aiptw" combines all three models into one estimating equation.
# Consistent if EITHER the Cox outcome model OR the propensity + censoring
# models are correctly specified, not necessarily both.
adj_aipw <- adjustedsurv(
  data            = sim_data,
  variable        = "treatment",
  ev_time         = "time",
  event           = "event",
  method          = "aiptw",
  conf_int        = TRUE,
  outcome_model   = cox_model,     # S(t | A, X)
  treatment_model = ps_model,      # P(A | X)
  censoring_model = cens_model     # P(C | X, A)
)

# ── 6. Plot adjusted survival curves ──────────────────────────────────────────
par(mfrow = c(1, 2))
plot(adj_ipw,  title = "IPTW-Adjusted Curves",
     xlab = "Time", ylab = "Survival Probability")
plot(adj_aipw, title = "AIPW-Adjusted Curves",
     xlab = "Time", ylab = "Survival Probability")

# ── 7. Restricted Mean Survival Time (RMST) ───────────────────────────────────
# RMST difference = area between the two counterfactual survival curves
# up to horizon tau. Interpretable: average months gained (or lost)
# attributable to treatment over a fixed follow-up window.
# As in Chapter V, `to` must fall within the latest observed EVENT time
# (method = "iptw_km" and "aiptw" both evaluate the adjusted curve only at
# observed event times), so tau is computed from the data rather than
# hard-coded.
latest_event_time <- max(sim_data$time[sim_data$event == 1])
tau <- floor(0.9 * latest_event_time)

rmst_ipw  <- adjusted_rmst(adj_ipw,  to = tau)
rmst_aipw <- adjusted_rmst(adj_aipw, to = tau)

print(rmst_ipw)
print(rmst_aipw)

A few implementation notes worth internalizing. First, always verify the weight distribution after fitting: print(adj_ipw) reports summary statistics for the estimated weights, and values consistently above 10-15 signal positivity violations that should be investigated before interpreting the results. Second, for AIPW, supply the censoring model whenever your study involves informative dropout; leaving censoring_model = NULL is equivalent to assuming \(P(C \mid X, A) = P(C)\), an assumption routinely violated in observational databases. The plain IPTW-KM estimator above has no equivalent argument to set in this package, which is exactly why it cannot be trusted on its own once dropout is suspected to depend on covariates. Third, for AIPW, use the same covariate sets across the three models where possible; asymmetric specifications can undermine the efficiency of the augmentation term even when both model pairs are technically correct.

Looking Ahead

AIPW gives us the most defensible estimate of the average causal effect, the population-level retention benefit or detriment attributable to treatment. But the average conceals heterogeneity that is often the most actionable finding. A retention offer adding two months of expected tenure on average may add eight months for new, low-engagement users and essentially nothing for customers who were already locked into a two-year contract; presenting the average to a marketing team deciding who to target answers the wrong question. Chapter VII turns to this directly, introducing conditional average treatment effects (CATE) and causal survival forests as the tools for mapping who benefits most from an intervention, the foundation of targeted retention spend.

Chapter VII. Heterogeneous Effects: Who Benefits More?

Every method we have used so far, from g-computation to AIPW, has been built to answer one number: the population average treatment effect. That number tells a CFO whether an intervention works. It tells almost nothing about how to allocate next quarter’s budget.

The Tyranny of the Average

Suppose a retention campaign produces an average RMST gain of 1.5 months over a 24-month horizon. A reasonable executive reads that as “the campaign buys 1.5 months of extra tenure per user, on average.” The average, however, is compatible with wildly different underlying realities. It is consistent with a world where every user gains roughly 1.5 months. It is just as consistent with a world where high-risk users (those flagged by a churn score in the top decile, say) gain 4 months, while low-risk users gain nothing at all.

If you only ever estimate the ATE, you cannot tell these two worlds apart, and that distinction is exactly the information a targeting strategy needs. Rolling the campaign out to the entire base is efficient in the first scenario. Doing the same in the second scenario means paying full price to move a number that, for most users, the intervention never actually touches. The conditional average treatment effect (CATE), written \(\tau(x)\), exists to recover that lost resolution.

From Causal Forests to Causal Survival Forests

The natural tool for estimating \(\tau(x)\) nonparametrically is the causal forest of Athey, Tibshirani, and Wager (2019), implemented in grf as causal_forest(). A causal forest grows trees that split on covariates likely to drive treatment effect heterogeneity, then uses the resulting adaptive neighborhoods to run a localized version of Robinson’s residual-on-residual regression at every point \(x\).

That machinery, as written, needs a fully observed outcome for every unit. In survival data we rarely have one. We only see \(U_i = T_i \wedge C_i\) and the event indicator \(\Delta_i\), and a meaningful fraction of users are still active (censored) at the time we pull the data.

Warning

A naive workaround here is the single most dangerous mistake in this chapter. Feeding causal_forest() the censored time \(U_i\) directly, or restricting the sample to completed cases only (\(\Delta_i = 1\)), does not produce a conservative or roughly right CATE. It produces a biased one. Censored users are, by construction, the ones who survived at least until \(U_i\), and treating \(U_i\) as their final outcome systematically understates their true survival time. If censoring rates differ across treatment arms or across regions of covariate space (which they almost always do in observational settings), this bias varies with \(x\) and contaminates exactly the heterogeneity signal you are trying to recover.

Cui, Kosorok, Sverdrup, Wager, and Zhu (2023) solve this with causal_survival_forest(), available in the grf package (Tibshirani et al., 2024). The function keeps the generalized random forest scaffolding (adaptive splitting, honesty, the bootstrap of little bags for confidence intervals) but replaces the splitting criterion with one built from doubly robust, Neyman-orthogonal estimating equations adapted from the semiparametric survival literature, with the full construction and asymptotic theory developed in Sverdrup & Wager (2024). In practice, every observation, censored or not, contributes to every candidate split through a pseudo-outcome that combines a weight derived from the estimated conditional survival function of the censoring process with an imputation term for what each censored user would likely have done. A simpler variant, also available in grf, reweights complete cases only using inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW); it is easier to reason about, but it throws away the partial information a censored observation still carries (this user had not churned by month nine, for instance) and it is not robust to errors in the censoring model. Log-rank splitting, by contrast, is a feature of classical random survival forests (Ishwaran, Kogalur, Blackstone & Lauer, 2008), not of causal survival forests, which target the treatment effect directly rather than the survival distribution itself. The doubly robust default is the one you should reach for unless you have a specific reason not to.

One caveat carries over directly from the population-level estimators in Chapters V and VI: a causal survival forest still needs covariate overlap between treatment arms to do its job. In a region of \(X\) where one arm has little or no support, the forest cannot estimate \(\hat\tau(x)\) from comparable neighbors, it extrapolates from whatever nearby observations exist, and the result can look like confident, well-defined heterogeneity while actually reflecting the adaptive tree structure more than the underlying data. The propensity overlap check from Chapter IX applies here too, just evaluated locally rather than only on average across the whole sample.

Defining the Conditional RMST

Formally, for a chosen horizon \(h\), the estimand is the conditional restricted mean survival time difference:

\[ \tau(x) = E\big[T^{(1)} - T^{(0)} \mid X = x\big] \]

or, in its truncated and identifiable form,

\[ \tau^h(x) = E\big[T^{(1)} \wedge h \mid X = x\big] - E\big[T^{(0)} \wedge h \mid X = x\big]. \]

This is the same RMST contrast established in Chapters I and IV, now indexed by \(x\) instead of averaged over the whole population. Each leaf of the forest behaves like a localized, doubly robust matched comparison: the adaptive weights \(\alpha_i(x)\) that grf computes internally tell you which training observations the forest considers comparable to a target user \(x\), and \(\hat\tau(x)\) is built from that weighted neighborhood using the same orthogonal score that protects the global ATE against censoring and confounding. The horizon \(h\) is not a free statistical convenience either. Pick it by looking at where censoring starts to dominate the data, the same way you would choose a truncation point for a population-level RMST in Chapter II’s terms, since behavior past that point is not identified from the sample in front of you.

Variable Importance and Targeting

A CATE forest does more than produce a number per user. Calling variable_importance() returns, for every covariate, a depth-weighted count of how often the forest split on it. High importance means the forest found that variable useful for separating users into groups with different treatment effects, which is a direct, data-driven map of where the heterogeneity actually lives.

This is where the chapter earns its keep on a P&L. Suppose the retention offer costs €50 per user (CRM outreach, agent time, the discount itself), and each extra month of retained tenure is worth €15 in margin. The break-even CATE is then €50 divided by €15, or about 3.3 months: below that gain, the offer destroys value, above it, the offer pays for itself. If variable importance points to tenure and weekly_logins as the dominant drivers of \(\hat\tau(x)\), the targeting rule writes itself: deploy the offer only to users with tenure < 3 months and low weekly_logins, where \(\hat\tau(x)\) clears the 3.3-month payback threshold. We cannot afford to target everyone at €50 a head, and the CATE is precisely the number that tells us where the spend is justified. Users outside that region get pulled from the rollout entirely, not because they don’t matter, but because the forest has told you, with a doubly robust guarantee, that the campaign does not move their survival curve enough to clear the bar.

To use it without overstating precision, evaluate it on a sample the CATE forest did not see, fitting a separate forest on the test fold for scoring, rather than reusing csf’s own training data twice.

We now have a complete toolkit (g-computation, AIPW, and causal survival forests), but applying each of these methods in isolation is not enough to ship a targeting decision into production. It is time to stitch them together into a single, end-to-end pipeline, built on a real-world customer churn dataset.

Chapter VIII. Full R Implementation: Customer Churn Case Study

Every dataset used so far in this post, the lung cohort in Chapter II, the simulated surv_df in Chapter III, the eight-row toy df in Chapter IV, the synthetic data in Chapter V, the simulated sim_data in Chapter VI, and the synthetic churn population in Chapter VII, was chosen to teach exactly one mechanical point as cleanly as possible, with no distractions from messy real-world covariates. That is a deliberate pedagogical choice, not an oversight: each method is easiest to understand on the dataset where its assumptions hold by construction. This chapter makes the opposite choice. It takes every method introduced so far and runs them, together, on a single real dataset, where assumptions are not guaranteed to hold and have to be checked rather than assumed.

Everything in Chapters II through VII has been building toward this moment. We have the Kaplan-Meier estimator, the log-rank test, the Cox model, and the potential-outcomes machinery for survival data. Now we put it all to work on a problem every subscription business recognizes: does offering a retention service actually keep customers around longer, or are we looking at a correlation that happens to wear a causal disguise? The VP of Data does not want a p-value on her desk. She wants a euro figure, and this chapter delivers one, showing exactly how naive and causal survival analysis can disagree by a wide margin.

Data Setup

We use the IBM Telco Customer Churn dataset (IBM, n.d.), a standard benchmark for subscription analytics with 7,043 customers. Our time variable is tenure, measured in months. The event is churn (recoded from “Yes”/“No” to 1/0). The intervention of interest is tech_support, a retention add-on that a customer either has or does not have. Covariates include contract (month-to-month, one-year, two-year), monthly_charges, phone_service, and internet_service. Tenure in this dataset tops out at 72 months, but the restriction horizon \(\tau\) is set just below the latest observed churn event rather than at 72 itself, since the adjusted curves used later in this chapter are only evaluated up to the last point at which an event actually occurred, not the last point at which any customer, churned or still active, was observed. Fixing \(\tau\) at the dataset’s nominal ceiling rather than at its actual evaluable support is a common source of silent extrapolation errors, and is exactly the kind of detail Chapter IX’s diagnostic checklist asks you to verify before trusting a number.

That alone is a good reminder of why RMST, not the median, is the workhorse summary for heavily censored data.

It would be tempting to stop here and tell the VP that TechSupport buys several extra months of customer lifetime. It does not. Customers who purchase TechSupport are not a random subset of the customer base. They are disproportionately on annual or two-year contracts, where the add-on is often bundled at a discount and where switching penalties already suppress churn on their own. Comparing raw curves conflates the effect of TechSupport with the effect of being a contractually sticky customer in the first place, which is precisely the confounding structure that makes naive comparisons untrustworthy, the same mechanism described narratively for the onboarding intervention in Chapter I.

Step 3: The Causal Shift (G-Computation via adjustedCurves)

G-computation works by using the outcome model we just fitted to generate, for every customer in the data, a predicted survival curve under both counterfactual scenarios, tech_support = "Yes" and tech_support = "No", and then averaging those curves over the empirical distribution of contract types, prices, and service bundles actually observed in our book of business. The adjustedCurves package implements this through adjustedsurv() with method = "direct", exactly the procedure derived in Chapter V.

Code 
adj_fit <- adjustedsurv(
  data          = churn_data,
  variable      = "tech_support",
  ev_time       = "tenure",
  event         = "event",
  method        = "direct",
  outcome_model = cox_fit,
  conf_int      = TRUE,
  bootstrap     = TRUE,
  n_boot        = 500
)

This step requires positivity, the assumption established in Chapter IV: every combination of contract type, price band, and service bundle must contain some customers with TechSupport and some without, or the standardization is forced to extrapolate into regions the data never observed. As a robustness check against misspecification of the outcome model, we also fit the augmented inverse probability weighted (AIPW) estimator from Chapter VI, which additionally requires a treatment model for who selects TechSupport.

Code 
treat_model <- glm(
  tech_support ~ contract + monthly_charges,
  data   = churn_data,
  family = binomial()
)

cens_model <- coxph(
  Surv(tenure, event == 0) ~ contract + monthly_charges +
    phone_service + internet_service,
  data = churn_data,
  x    = TRUE
)

adj_fit_aiptw <- adjustedsurv(
  data            = churn_data,
  variable        = "tech_support",
  ev_time         = "tenure",
  event           = "event",
  method          = "aiptw",
  outcome_model   = cox_fit,
  treatment_model = treat_model,
  censoring_model = cens_model,
  conf_int        = TRUE,
  bootstrap       = TRUE,
  n_boot          = 500
)

AIPW is doubly robust: it remains consistent if either the outcome model or the treatment model is correctly specified, but not necessarily both, exactly the property derived in Chapter VI. Agreement between adj_fit and adj_fit_aiptw is the standard pre-deployment sanity check. If the two disagree by more than a few tenths of a month, we have a specification problem that must be resolved before going to the business.

Step 4: The Business Answer (Causal RMST)

With the adjusted curves in hand, we compute the causal RMST for each arm by integrating the area under the counterfactual survival curve up to \(\tau\), the same operation defined formally in Chapters IV and V.

\[ \text{RMST}_\tau(a) = \int_0^\tau S^{(a)}(t)\, dt, \quad a \in \{\text{No}, \text{Yes}\} \]

Code 
rmst_adj <- adjusted_rmst(adj_fit, from = 0, to = tau)
print(rmst_adj)

rmst_diff <- rmst_adj$rmst[rmst_adj$group == "Yes"] -
             rmst_adj$rmst[rmst_adj$group == "No"]
rmst_diff

A typical result on this dataset is a causal RMST difference of roughly 1.4 months. Of the 5 to 6 months we saw in the naive curves, only about a quarter survives adjustment for contract type, price, and service bundle. Most of the apparent effect was confounding, not causation. Translated into euros, at an average revenue per user of €50 per month:

Code 
arpu        <- 50
clv_uplift  <- rmst_diff * arpu
clv_uplift

This is a €70 increase in customer lifetime value per customer who takes up the service (\(1.4 \text{ months} \times €50/\text{month} = €70\)). Scaled across 10,000 eligible subscribers, that is \(€70 \times 10{,}000 = €700{,}000\) in portfolio value: a real but far more modest number than the naive analysis would have promised, and one the business can defend in front of a skeptical CFO.

A technical detail matters here and is easy to miss. For method = "direct", no analytic standard error has been derived for the RMST difference in the literature, so the confidence interval around rmst_diff must come from the bootstrap replicates generated by bootstrap = TRUE, not from naively subtracting the two marginal confidence intervals reported for each arm. Treating the two-arm CIs as independent and differencing them ignores the correlation between the counterfactual curves and will misstate the uncertainty, almost always in the optimistic direction.

Chapter IX. A Diagnostic Checklist for Causal Survival Analysis

Chapter VIII produced a euro figure. This chapter is about earning the right to trust it. Every method in this post rests on assumptions, proportional hazards for the Cox-based outcome model, positivity for both treatment and censoring, correct specification of at least one model in the doubly robust case, and none of those assumptions check themselves. This chapter collects the diagnostics in one place, organized the way you would actually use them: as a checklist to run before you put a number in front of a stakeholder, not as an afterthought once the number already exists.

When to Use G-Computation vs. IPW vs. AIPW vs. Causal Survival Forests

The four estimators developed across Chapters V, VI, and VII are not interchangeable, and the choice among them should follow from what you know about your data, not from which package happens to have the friendliest documentation.

Method Models required Robust to Best when Avoid when
G-computation Outcome model (Cox) Nothing, if outcome model is wrong Confounders are well understood and the outcome model is plausible (Chapter V) You are unsure the Cox model’s functional form or PH assumption holds
IPW (IPTW + IPCW) Treatment model, censoring model Nothing, if either weighting model is wrong The treatment assignment mechanism is well understood and propensity scores are not extreme (Chapter VI) Propensity scores cluster near 0 or 1, or censoring is poorly modeled
AIPW Outcome model, treatment model, censoring model Misspecification of either the outcome model or the pair of weighting models (Chapter VI) You want insurance against not knowing in advance which model is right You have neither the data nor the modelling resources to fit three models reliably
Causal survival forest None specified parametrically; learned by the forest Misspecification of any single parametric model, via the same doubly robust orthogonal score as AIPW You suspect meaningful treatment effect heterogeneity and want to target a subpopulation (Chapter VII) You only need the population ATE, sample size is small, or interpretability of a single coefficient matters to the audience

A practical decision rule: start with g-computation as a fast, transparent baseline because it requires only one model. Cross-check it with AIPW before reporting any number externally, since agreement between the two is the cheapest robustness check available, exactly the comparison performed in Chapter VIII between adj_fit and adj_fit_aiptw. Reach for a causal survival forest only once the population-level question has been answered and the next question on the table is “who should we target,” not “does this work.”

Checking the Proportional Hazards Assumption in Practice

If any part of the pipeline uses a Cox model, outcome model, propensity model, or censoring model, the proportional hazards assumption deserves the same scrutiny in the case study as it received in the toy example of Chapter III. The mechanics do not change: fit the model, then run

Code 
ph_check <- cox.zph(cox_fit, transform = "km")
print(ph_check)
plot(ph_check)

A global p-value below 0.05, or any individual covariate’s p-value below 0.05, is reason to inspect the corresponding Schoenfeld residual plot rather than report the hazard ratio as a stable summary. In the Chapter VIII case study, this check should be run on cox_fit before the hazard ratio in Step 2 is interpreted as a constant 15 to 20% reduction at every point in tenure. If tech_support’s effect decays over the customer lifecycle, exactly the pattern motivated narratively in Chapter III’s discussion of the day-7 notification, a single hazard ratio understates the early effect and overstates the late one, even though the g-computation and AIPW estimates in Step 3 and Step 4 remain valid regardless, since RMST carries no proportional hazards assumption to violate.

Checking Positivity

Positivity is the assumption most often skipped in practice and most often the actual reason a causal survival result is wrong. Two distinct positivity assumptions were stated formally in Chapter IV, treatment positivity and censoring positivity, and both need a check, not just a citation.

For treatment positivity, plot the estimated propensity scores separately by treatment arm:

Code 
ps_hat <- predict(treat_model, type = "response")

library(ggplot2)
ggplot(data.frame(ps = ps_hat, arm = churn_data$tech_support),
       aes(x = ps, fill = arm)) +
  geom_histogram(position = "identity", alpha = 0.5, bins = 40) +
  labs(title = "Propensity score overlap by treatment arm",
       x = "Estimated P(tech_support = Yes | X)", y = "Count") +
  theme_minimal()

Good overlap looks like two histograms that substantially cover the same range. A positivity violation looks like one arm piling up near 0 or 1 with little support from the other arm at the same covariate values, the customer profiles that exist in the data but for which one treatment level is never, or almost never, observed. Trimming the tails (dropping observations outside the region of common support) or switching to overlap weighting are the standard remedies; neither fixes the underlying data limitation, both make the resulting estimate honest about what it can and cannot speak to.

For censoring positivity, the check is structurally identical but on the censoring model: examine \(\hat{G}(t \mid X, A)\) for each treatment arm and confirm it does not collapse toward zero before \(\tau\) within any covariate stratum. If certain contract types or service bundles are censored almost universally before the horizon of interest, the RMST in that stratum is not actually identified from the data, no matter how confident the point estimate looks.

Reporting RMST vs. the Hazard Ratio to a Non-Technical Stakeholder

The choice of which number to lead with is not cosmetic, it determines whether the audience walks away with an accurate mental model. As Chapter II established, a hazard ratio of 0.82 requires the listener to first understand what a hazard is, then accept that it is constant over time (an assumption they cannot evaluate themselves), and only then translate an abstract relative risk reduction into something resembling a business outcome. An RMST difference of 1.4 months at a chosen horizon \(\tau\) skips all three steps. It is already in the units the stakeholder thinks in, and as Chapter VIII demonstrated, it multiplies directly into a euro figure.

The practical recommendation is to lead with RMST and the resulting CLV calculation in any executive-facing material, and to keep the hazard ratio, if it is reported at all, as a supporting technical detail for an audience that will ask about it specifically (typically other analysts, or a CFO’s own data science function performing due diligence). Never present the hazard ratio alone as if it settled the business question; Chapter III’s central argument was precisely that it does not.

Conclusion

This post opened with a VP of Data who compared two churn rates, declared victory, and triggered a board-approved rollout, and it has spent nine chapters making the case that her instinct, while completely understandable, was not safe to act on. The central thesis has not changed since Chapter I: the difference between descriptive and causal survival analysis is not an academic distinction that matters only to statisticians defending a dissertation. It is the difference between a retention budget that is spent where it actually moves the needle and one that is spent paying for a correlation that would have happened anyway.

Chapter II gave that thesis its vocabulary: the survival function, the hazard function, and the restricted mean survival time, the descriptive tools that every causal claim in this post was eventually built on top of. Chapter III explained, with the precision the claim deserves, why the most common shortcut, an adjusted Cox hazard ratio, is not a causal estimand even when every observable confounder has been included and even inside a randomized trial. That is the reason this entire post exists, not a minor caveat tacked on at the end. Chapter IV then did the harder and less glamorous work of stating, formally, what would have to be true for a causal claim about time-to-event data to be identifiable at all: potential outcomes, SUTVA, unconfoundedness, and two distinct positivity conditions, one for treatment and one, easily forgotten, for censoring.

Chapters V through VII turned those assumptions into estimators. G-computation is the most transparent of the three and the right place to start, provided you trust your outcome model. AIPW is the right place to land once a number is about to leave the building, because it survives the failure of either the outcome model or the weighting models, not just one specific guess about which model would be correct. Causal survival forests answer a different and, for a budget-constrained team, often more actionable question: not “does this work,” but “for whom does this work enough to justify the spend.” Chapter VIII showed all three working together on a real dataset, and the headline number, a causal RMST gain of roughly 1.4 months, or about €70 in customer lifetime value, was honest in a way the naive 5-to-6-month gap was not: smaller, more defensible, and survivable in front of a CFO who has seen optimistic projections fail before.

Chapter IX and the five questions that followed exist because none of this is self-certifying. A causal survival curve is only as trustworthy as the diagnostics that were actually run against it, proportional hazards checked rather than assumed, positivity inspected as a histogram rather than cited as a footnote, and at least two estimators compared rather than one estimator trusted by default. The gap between “we ran adjustedsurv()” and “we ran adjustedsurv(), checked cox.zph(), inspected the propensity overlap, and confirmed the g-computation and AIPW estimates agree within a defensible margin” is the entire difference between a number that survives scrutiny and one that does not.

None of the machinery in this post is exotic. The survival package has existed for decades, adjustedCurves and grf are both actively maintained, peer-reviewed, and free. What is still comparatively rare is the discipline to run the full pipeline, naive curve, adjusted curve, diagnostic checklist, euro translation, rather than stopping at whichever step first produces a number that confirms what the team already believed. That discipline, more than any single estimator, is what separates an analysis a business can act on from one that merely looks like it can.

## The Missing Ingredient in Most Churn Models

A SaaS company launches a retention intervention, personalized onboarding emails, a dedicated account manager, and a targeted discount offer. After six months, the data team reports something striking: among the 2,400 users who received the intervention, 12% churned. Among the 8,600 who did not, 23% churned. The VP of Data presents these numbers to the executive team and declares the intervention a clear success. The board approves a full rollout. Nobody asks the only question that matters: *would those same users have churned at 12% even without the intervention?*

They might have. The retention team did not assign the intervention at random; they targeted users who were already logging in regularly, exploring new features, and engaging with support. These users were less likely to churn *regardless* of whether anyone intervened. Prior engagement caused both selection into the intervention and lower churn. The 11-percentage-point gap is real, but it conflates the effect of the intervention with the effect of being the kind of user who gets selected for one. In causal language, engagement is a confounder: it sits on a backdoor path from intervention to churn, and failing to block that path makes the raw comparison uninformative about cause and effect.

This confusion is not unique to SaaS. Any organization that uses time-to-event outcomes, customer churn, employee turnover, loan default, equipment failure, faces the same trap when evaluating interventions. Standard descriptive tools tell you what happened to those who received a treatment. They do not tell you what would have happened had you assigned the treatment differently. The gap between these two questions is the subject of causal survival analysis.

### Why Adjusting for Covariates Does Not Fix It

A well-trained analyst will object at this point and propose running a Cox proportional hazards regression, adjusting for login frequency, subscription tier, account age, and whatever else is measured. The adjusted hazard ratio will look more credible than the raw gap. But it does not solve the problem, for a reason that is deeper than confounding and that persists even in randomized trials.

The hazard ratio is not a clean causal estimand, even after adjustment, and even in the absence of unmeasured confounding (Hernán, 2010). There are two reasons for this, and the second is more fundamental than the first.

The first problem is that the hazard ratio can change over time. A Cox model typically reports a single number, a weighted average of time-specific ratios, and that average depends on how long the study runs. The same underlying reality could yield a hazard ratio of 1.8 at six months and something close to 1.0 at two years. A single summary statistic papers over a potentially meaningful time-varying effect.

The second problem is structural. The hazard at time $t$ is defined as the instantaneous risk of churn among users who have *not yet churned by* $t$. Surviving to $t$ is itself affected by the intervention, which means the hazard ratio at $t$ compares two groups, treated survivors and untreated survivors, that have been made incomparable by the act of conditioning on post-treatment survival. Over time, the treated group is selectively depleted of high-risk users who churned early, so the remaining treated users become systematically different from the remaining untreated users on unobserved susceptibility. A harmful intervention can appear protective at later time points simply because it has already eliminated the most vulnerable users from the risk set. The hazard ratio answers how the instantaneous risk compares between groups *conditional on survival up to that moment*, not how total survival time would change under a different treatment assignment. These are different questions, and the former does not generally imply the latter (Hernán, 2010). No amount of covariate adjustment within the Cox framework removes this built-in selection bias. The full formal argument is deferred to Chapter III, but the practical implication is immediate: if your effect measure can make a harmful intervention look protective, something is wrong with the measure.

### The Counterfactual Question

What the VP of Data needed was not a better descriptive summary of two incomparable groups. She needed to answer a strictly counterfactual question: what would the churn rate have been had we assigned the intervention to *every* user, versus to *no* user? Not what happened to the users who received it, but what would have happened in a world where assignment was determined by policy rather than by engagement history.

Formally, let $T^{(1)}$ denote the potential time-to-churn under universal treatment and $T^{(0)}$ the potential time-to-churn under no treatment. The causal effect on the restricted mean survival time (RMST) over a horizon $\tau$ is

$$\tau_{\text{RMST}} \;=\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[T^{(1)} \wedge \tau\right] - \mathbb{E}\!\left[T^{(0)} \wedge \tau\right] \;=\; \int_0^{\tau} \!\left(S^{(1)}(t) - S^{(0)}(t)\right) dt$$

where $S^{(a)}(t) = P(T^{(a)} > t)$ is the counterfactual survival function under treatment arm $a$, and $a \wedge b = \min(a, b)$. This estimand is the area between two counterfactual survival curves. It requires no proportional-hazards assumption, carries no built-in selection bias, and answers the decision-maker's question directly: how many additional months, on average, does the intervention keep a user subscribed over the window we care about?

The gap between the descriptive question ("who churned less?") and the causal question ("what would happen if we intervened on everyone?") is not academic. Business decisions are themselves counterfactuals: they concern what will happen when something is *changed*, not what already happened under the status quo. If a model does not answer a counterfactual question, it cannot justify a business decision. Without the tools of causal survival analysis, any claim about the intervention's effect remains speculation dressed in statistics.

## Chapter II. What Survival Analysis Actually Estimates {#sec-ch2}

### Why Standard Regression Fails {#sec-why-ols-fails}

A natural first instinct when studying customer churn is to reach for ordinary least squares: regress time-to-churn on a set of covariates and read off the coefficients. Or perhaps discretize the problem, churned by month twelve versus not, and fit a logistic model. Both instincts are understandable, and both fail in systematic ways that are worth naming precisely.

The first problem is structural: survival times are almost always right-skewed, often heavily so, and OLS assumes roughly symmetric errors. Predictions can be negative, confidence intervals are distorted, and inference is inefficient. But the second problem is the decisive one. At any fixed analysis date, many subjects have not yet experienced the event. A customer who signed up three months ago and is still active contributes real information, her lifetime exceeds three months, but that information is incomplete. We do not know when she will churn. This is **right-censoring**, and OLS has no principled mechanism for handling it. Dropping censored observations introduces selection bias, since longer-tenured customers are precisely those most likely to be censored. Treating the censored time as the true event time biases every estimate downward. Neither option is acceptable.

Formally, for subject $i$ we observe the pair $(\tilde{T}_i, \Delta_i)$, where

$$\tilde{T}_i = \min(T_i,\, C_i)$$

is the observed time, $T_i$ is the latent true event time, $C_i$ is the censoring time, and $\Delta_i = \mathbf{1}(T_i \leq C_i)$ is the event indicator. The critical identifying assumption is **non-informative censoring**: $T_i \perp C_i \mid \mathbf{X}_i$, meaning that the reason a subject is censored carries no information about when they would have experienced the event, conditional on covariates. Under this assumption, a censored observation at time $\tilde{T}_i$ tells us unambiguously that $T_i > \tilde{T}_i$, and this partial information can be exploited correctly. Survival analysis is the set of tools that does exactly this. This notation, $\tilde{T}_i$ for the observed time and $\Delta_i$ for the event indicator, is used throughout the rest of this post.

Logistic regression fares no better than OLS. It forces an arbitrary dichotomization of the time axis and discards the ordering information that makes time-to-event data so rich. A customer who churns on day five and one who churns on day two hundred and ninety are treated identically if both fall before the chosen cutoff. That is not a model of churn, it is a compression of churn into something that ignores its most important feature.

### The Survival Function $S(t)$ and the Hazard Function $h(t)$ {#sec-survival-hazard}

Two complementary mathematical objects sit at the core of time-to-event analysis.

The **survival function** is defined as

$$S(t) = P(T > t), \quad t \geq 0.$$

It gives the probability that a subject has not yet experienced the event by time $t$. For a SaaS platform, $S(t)$ is the retention curve: the proportion of a customer cohort still active at month $t$. It satisfies $S(0) = 1$, is non-increasing, and in theory approaches zero as $t \to \infty$, though in practice we observe it only down to some positive value determined by the length of follow-up. If $T$ is continuous with density $f(t)$ and CDF $F(t)$, then $S(t) = 1 - F(t) = \int_t^{\infty} f(u)\, du$. A value of $S(12) = 0.68$ means that 68% of customers are still active twelve months after sign-up, a concrete, immediately interpretable retention figure.

The **hazard function** answers a different question: not how many customers remain, but at what rate are those who remain leaving right now? Formally,

$$h(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{P(t \leq T < t + \Delta t \mid T \geq t)}{\Delta t}.$$

This is an instantaneous conditional rate, not a probability. For a small interval $\Delta t$, the quantity $h(t)\,\Delta t$ approximates the probability that a customer who is still active at month $t$ churns before month $t + \Delta t$. A value of $h(6) = 0.04$ means that among customers who have survived to month six, roughly 4% churn per month at that moment. Think of $h(t)$ as the churn pressure currently being exerted on the surviving base: it can be high in the first weeks after onboarding (early friction), fall as loyal customers reveal themselves, and potentially rise again as contracts approach renewal dates.

The two functions are not independent. They are linked by the identity

$$S(t) = \exp\!\left(-\int_0^t h(u)\, du\right) = \exp(-H(t)),$$

where $H(t) = \int_0^t h(u)\, du$ is the **cumulative hazard**. This relationship has a clean interpretation: the survival probability at time $t$ equals the exponentiated negative sum of all instantaneous churn pressures accumulated from the start up to $t$. Every month of elevated hazard compounds against the survival curve, and the curve is the running product of surviving those accumulated risks.

### The Kaplan-Meier Estimator {#sec-kaplan-meier}

Given data $(\tilde{T}_i, \Delta_i)$ for $i = 1, \ldots, n$, the challenge is to estimate $S(t)$ without imposing a parametric form on the event time distribution. The **Kaplan-Meier estimator** is the non-parametric maximum likelihood solution. Let $t_{(1)} < t_{(2)} < \cdots < t_{(k)}$ be the ordered distinct event times observed in the sample, $d_j$ the number of events at time $t_{(j)}$, and $n_j$ the number of subjects still at risk (under follow-up and event-free) just before $t_{(j)}$. The estimator is

$$\hat{S}(t) = \prod_{j:\, t_{(j)} \leq t} \left(1 - \frac{d_j}{n_j}\right).$$

The logic is multiplicative: at each event time, the conditional probability of surviving that instant is $(n_j - d_j)/n_j$, and the survival probability at $t$ is the product of all such conditional probabilities up to $t$. Censored observations contribute to the risk set $n_j$ for all times up to their censoring point and then exit, they never appear in the numerator $d_j$ but they support the denominator for earlier intervals, which is exactly how their partial information enters the estimate (Hosmer, Lemeshow & May, 2008, Chapters 1-2). The result is a right-continuous step function that drops only at observed event times and remains flat between them.

For a customer cohort, the Kaplan-Meier curve is the empirical retention curve, and it is more honest than naive proportion-remaining calculations precisely because it accounts correctly for customers observed over different lengths of time. It is the natural descriptive baseline before any modelling begins.

### Restricted Mean Survival Time {#sec-rmst}

The Kaplan-Meier curve is visually informative but stakeholders routinely want a single summary number: how long, on average, does a customer stay? The naive mean survival time is undefined when the survival curve does not reach zero, which is the typical case when follow-up is finite and some subjects are censored, because the integral $\int_0^\infty \hat{S}(t)\, dt$ diverges. The **Restricted Mean Survival Time (RMST)** resolves this by truncating the integral at a pre-specified horizon $\tau$:

$$\text{RMST}(\tau) = \int_0^{\tau} S(t)\, dt.$$

Geometrically, $\text{RMST}(\tau)$ is the area under the survival curve from zero to $\tau$. If $\tau = 24$ months and $\text{RMST}(24) = 17.4$, the interpretation is direct: a customer is expected to remain active for 17.4 of the first 24 months after sign-up. That sentence can be delivered in a board presentation and immediately converted into expected revenue per customer.

Contrast this with the **hazard ratio** produced by a Cox proportional hazards model. A hazard ratio of 0.72 says that the treated group experiences a 28% reduction in the instantaneous churn rate relative to control. This is a relative measure on an abstract scale. It does not tell you whether the improvement translates into two additional days of customer lifetime or two additional years, that depends entirely on the baseline hazard, which the hazard ratio does not report. A decision-maker weighing the cost of an intervention against its benefit needs an absolute quantity in interpretable units. $\Delta\,\text{RMST}(\tau) = 1.5$ months means the intervention extends expected active lifetime by 1.5 months within the two-year window. That figure, multiplied by average monthly revenue, is a CLV calculation. A hazard ratio cannot do that arithmetic directly.

A second advantage of RMST is robustness. The hazard ratio is only interpretable as a constant if the proportional hazards assumption holds, that the ratio $h_1(t)/h_0(t)$ is the same at every time $t$. This assumption fails routinely in practice, particularly when treatment effects evolve over time or when subgroups have crossing survival curves. The RMST requires no such assumption. It is defined and estimable under any survival distribution, which makes it an attractive target for causal inference, as we will develop in Chapter III.

### R Implementation {#sec-r-km}

The following code uses the `survival` package to define a survival object, fit Kaplan-Meier curves, and extract the RMST. We use the `lung` dataset, a standard teaching example holding survival times in days for patients with advanced lung cancer from the North Central Cancer Treatment Group, stratified by sex. This is the first of several illustrative datasets used across this post to teach one method at a time as cleanly as possible; Chapter VIII transitions explicitly to a single real customer churn dataset once every method has been introduced.

#| fig-cap: "Kaplan-Meier retention curves by sex. Tick marks indicate censored observations. The shaded bands are pointwise 95% confidence intervals."

# ── 1. Define the Surv object ─────────────────────────────────────────────
# lung$status: 1 = censored, 2 = dead. We recode to the standard 0/1 convention.

# sex: 1 = male, 2 = female

summary(km_fit, times = c(180, 365, 547))

plot(
  km_fit,
  col      = c("#2166AC", "#D6604D"),  # blue = male, red = female
  lwd      = 2,
  conf.int = TRUE,
  mark.time = TRUE,   # tick marks at censoring times

  ylab     = expression(hat(S)(t)),
  main     = "Kaplan-Meier curves by sex: NCCTG Lung Cancer"

legend(
  "topright",
  legend = c("Male (sex = 1)", "Female (sex = 2)"),
  col    = c("#2166AC", "#D6604D"),

If males show an RMST of 308 days and females 453 days at $\tau = 730$, the gap is 145 days, an absolute, units-based quantity. Female patients are expected to survive roughly five additional months within the two-year window. The standard errors allow a straightforward group comparison without the proportional hazards assumption that a log-rank test or Cox model would impose.

## Choosing $\tau$

The truncation horizon $\tau$ should be set to a time point well within the observed follow-up of all groups, so that $\hat{S}(\tau)$ is reliably estimated. A common choice is the minimum of the largest observed event time across groups. Setting $\tau$ too large inflates variance; setting it too small discards information. In business applications, $\tau$ often corresponds naturally to a contract horizon, a fiscal year, or the length of a planned experiment.
:::

### What These Tools Cannot Do {#sec-limits}

Every quantity defined and estimated in this chapter, $S(t)$, $h(t)$, the Kaplan-Meier curve, and the RMST, is **purely descriptive**. These tools characterize the observed survival experience of a group under study. They do not adjust for confounding, and they do not identify causal effects. A Kaplan-Meier curve stratified by treatment group conflates the effect of the treatment with any pre-existing differences between the groups that influenced who received it. The log-rank test detects a difference between groups without attributing it to a cause. The Cox proportional hazards model conditions on covariates, but its coefficients remain associational unless a credible identification strategy underlies the analysis, a strategy the model itself does not supply.

To move from "customers who received the onboarding intervention stay longer" to "the intervention *causes* customers to stay longer," we need the causal inference layer, and that layer must contend with the specific complications that time introduces: time-varying confounders, selection on survival, and the non-collapsibility of the hazard ratio. Chapter III builds that identification architecture, explaining why the log-rank test and the standard Cox model are insufficient for causal claims and introducing the estimands and assumptions that support valid causal survival analysis.

## Chapter III. Why the Log-Rank Test Is Not Enough {#sec-logrank}

Chapters I and II gave you the descriptive vocabulary of survival analysis: Kaplan-Meier curves, cumulative hazard functions, the machinery for handling censoring. That vocabulary is genuinely useful, but it is also quietly dangerous. It is possible to run a log-rank test, fit a Cox model, obtain a hazard ratio of 0.85, and walk into a leadership meeting with a number that looks like an answer. This chapter is about why that number is probably not the answer you think it is, and in some settings, not an answer at all.

### The Log-Rank Test Cannot See Confounding {#sec-logrank-confounding}

The log-rank test compares two observed Kaplan-Meier curves. Under the null hypothesis that the two survival functions are identical, it constructs a weighted sum of the differences between observed and expected event counts at each event time, and tests whether that sum is distinguishable from zero. The test is nonparametric and distribution-free, which makes it elegant. It is also, in the precise statistical sense, *unadjusted*.

What this means in practice is that the log-rank test compares groups as they appear in the data, not as they would appear in an ideal experiment. Suppose a subscription platform offers a discount to users who click a re-engagement banner. Those users are more engaged to begin with: they open emails, explore features, and were unlikely to churn regardless of the discount. The superior retention observed in the treated group is then a mixture of the campaign's effect and the pre-existing advantages of whoever self-selected into it. The log-rank test has no mechanism for disentangling these contributions. It measures association between treatment status and survival, full stop. When treatment assignment is confounded by covariates that also affect the time-to-event outcome, the test statistic will be inflated, or deflated, by exactly that confounding, and there is no algebraic device inside the test that can correct for it.

The problem is not ignorance of confounding on the part of statisticians who use the log-rank test. The problem is that the test was designed to answer a descriptive question (do these two groups differ in survival experience?) and it is routinely recruited to answer a causal question (does the treatment change survival?). Those are not the same question, and conflating them is the foundational error of naive survival analysis.

### The Cox Model as an Associational Tool {#sec-cox-associational}

The natural reflex is to reach for the Cox proportional hazards model, which adjusts for observed confounders and so appears to solve the identification problem. The standard Cox model specifies the conditional hazard for individual $i$ as

$$
h(t \mid X_i, A_i) = h_0(t) \exp(X_i^\top \beta + \gamma A_i),
$$

where $h_0(t)$ is an unspecified baseline hazard, $X_i$ is a vector of observed confounders, and $A_i \in \{0, 1\}$ is the treatment indicator. The coefficient $\gamma$ yields an *adjusted* hazard ratio $\exp(\gamma)$ that conditions on $X$. This is better than the unadjusted log-rank comparison.

But "adjusted" is not the same as "causal," and the distinction matters enormously. The hazard ratio that Cox produces is a *conditional* parameter: it compares the instantaneous risk of the event at time $t$ for two individuals who share the same covariate values $X$ but differ in treatment. The quantity of interest for causal inference is a *marginal* parameter: the effect of assigning the entire population to treatment versus control, averaging over the distribution of $X$ in that population. The Cox model conditions on $X$ but does not marginalize over it.

As Voinot et al. (2025) make explicit, the hazard ratio derived from a Cox model does not represent a causal contrast even in randomized trials, due to selection bias that accumulates in the risk sets over time. This is not a failure of implementation; it is a structural property of the hazard ratio as an estimand. There is additionally the problem of *non-collapsibility*: even under correct specification and no unmeasured confounding, the conditional hazard ratio from a Cox model will systematically differ from the marginal hazard ratio in the population, because the hazard ratio is a non-collapsible measure (Huitfeldt, Stensrud & Suzuki, 2019). The marginal causal effect cannot be recovered by averaging the conditional estimates across covariate strata. The adjusted Cox HR is not a stepping stone to the causal effect; it is a fundamentally different object.

### The Proportional Hazards Assumption {#sec-ph-assumption}

Beneath both problems sits a third, which compounds them. The Cox model does not simply estimate a hazard ratio; it assumes that the hazard ratio is *constant over time*. Formally, the proportional hazards (PH) assumption states that

$$
\frac{h(t \mid A = 1, X)}{h(t \mid A = 0, X)} = \exp(\gamma) \quad \text{for all } t,
$$

where the right-hand side is a constant independent of $t$. If a treated user has a 15% lower instantaneous churn hazard than an untreated user at day 30, the PH assumption requires that same 15% reduction to hold at day 90, day 180, and day 365. The two groups' hazard functions are parallel on the log scale for all time, forever.

That is a strong assumption. Grambsch & Therneau (1994) showed that non-proportionality can be expressed as a time-varying coefficient: if the true hazard is $h(t \mid A_i) = h_0(t) \exp\{\beta(t) A_i\}$, then PH is simply the restriction $\beta(t) = \gamma$ for all $t$. Violations occur whenever $\beta(t)$ trends, jumps, or decays, and they are far more common in business data than in the clinical trials for which Cox's model was originally designed.

Think concretely about a retention intervention: an in-app notification sent to users on day 7 of a free trial. The effect of that nudge is almost certainly largest in the first two or three weeks, when it changes immediate behavior. By month 6, users who stayed have either converted or are deeply habituated; the notification from five months ago is no longer a meaningful driver of their current survival probability. The hazard ratio starts large in magnitude and decays toward null as time passes. Any model that assumes otherwise is not just slightly wrong; it is misspecified in a way that systematically distorts every downstream inference.

### The Danger of a Single Summary Measure {#sec-single-hr}

When the PH assumption fails, the Cox model does not produce an error. It continues to produce a coefficient, a standard error, and a confidence interval. What it produces is a *time-weighted average* of a heterogeneous effect, and that average is deeply misleading.

This is the central argument of Hernán (2010). Using the Women's Health Initiative randomized trial of hormone therapy, he shows that the reported average hazard ratio of 1.24 was composed of year-specific ratios of 1.81, 1.34, 1.27, 1.25, 1.45, and 0.70 for successive years of follow-up. The single summary of 1.24 would have been approximately 1.80 if the trial had been halted after year one, or close to 1.00 if followed long enough for the declining trend to average out. The 24% increase in coronary heart disease risk that made international headlines was, in part, an artifact of the arbitrary choice of follow-up duration, not a stable property of the treatment.

The business translation is direct. A VP of Data who sees $\hat{\gamma} \rightarrow \exp(\hat{\gamma}) = 0.85$ will assume a constant 15% reduction in churn risk at every point in the customer lifecycle. If instead the intervention had a large effect in weeks one through three and essentially no effect thereafter, the "0.85" is an average of a time-varying $\beta(t)$ that ranged from, say, 0.60 in week one to 0.98 by month six. Planning retention budget based on a constant effect that does not actually exist at scale is not a minor analytical error; it is a strategic misallocation built on a statistical artifact.

Detecting this problem requires testing. The canonical diagnostic is `cox.zph()` from the `survival` package, which implements the Grambsch & Therneau (1994) score test. The test regresses the scaled Schoenfeld residuals, defined at each event time $t_k$ as $r_k(\hat{\gamma}) = A_{(k)} - M(\hat{\gamma}, t_k)$, where $A_{(k)}$ is the treatment indicator of the subject who experienced the event and $M(\hat{\gamma}, t_k)$ is the risk-weighted mean treatment at $t_k$, against a function of time. Under the PH assumption, the scaled residuals should have zero trend. A significant slope is evidence of nonproportionality. Plotting the smoothed scaled residuals against event times reveals the functional form of $\beta(t)$: whether the treatment effect grows, decays, or reverses direction.

### Two Failures, One Trap {#sec-two-failures}

Stepping back, the standard survival toolkit presents two distinct but compounding failures for causal inference. The first is *confounding*: when treatment is not randomly assigned, the log-rank test and the Cox model capture selection into treatment, not the effect of treatment. Adding covariates to the Cox model reduces this problem but does not solve it, because the conditional hazard ratio is not the same quantity as the marginal causal effect, and because unmeasured confounders are simply absent from the model. The second failure is *temporal heterogeneity*: even when confounding is absent, the PH assumption licenses the compression of a potentially rich, time-varying effect into a single number that may accurately describe no particular moment in the follow-up period.

What is conspicuously absent from the standard toolkit is any formal language for counterfactual reasoning. The log-rank test and the Cox model describe what happened to groups we observed. They have no native vocabulary for asking what *would have happened* to a unit under a treatment it did not receive, the foundational question of causal inference. Standard survival analysis describes what was observed, not what would have happened under a different policy.

Those two questions have different answers, and conflating them is why a single hazard ratio from a business survival analysis can be simultaneously precise and causally meaningless.

Standard survival analysis lacks the vocabulary to answer causal questions about censored time-to-event outcomes. Chapter IV builds that vocabulary from the ground up, introducing the potential outcomes framework adapted for survival data, defining counterfactual event times $T^{(1)}$ and $T^{(0)}$, stating the identifying assumptions that connect them to what we observe, and motivating the restricted mean survival time as a causal estimand that remains interpretable even when proportional hazards fails.

## Chapter IV. Potential Outcomes Meet Censored Data

Causal inference requires comparing two worlds that cannot both be observed. Survival analysis compounds this problem by adding a second source of missingness: the event may not have occurred yet when we stop watching. Before we can estimate anything useful, we need to formalize exactly what we are trying to learn and what we are willing to assume.

### Potential Outcomes for Survival Times

<!-- EDITOR'S NOTE: The source chapters used three different notations for the same potential

Following the Rubin Causal Model (Rubin, 1974), attach to each individual $i$ two *potential* event times:

- $T_i^{(1)}$: the time at which individual $i$ would experience the event if assigned to treatment ($A_i = 1$).
- $T_i^{(0)}$: the time at which individual $i$ would experience the event if assigned to control ($A_i = 0$).

These are not sequential observations. They are parallel counterfactual quantities, only one of which can ever be realized. This is the fundamental problem of causal inference applied to survival data (Hernán & Robins, 2020, Chapter 17). We observe:

$$T_i = A_i \cdot T_i^{(1)} + (1 - A_i) \cdot T_i^{(0)}$$

where $A_i \in \{0,1\}$ is the treatment indicator. For every individual, one potential outcome is structurally missing. A treated customer never reveals how long they would have retained under the control condition. A control customer never reveals how quickly they would have churned had they received the onboarding intervention.

The situation is further complicated by censoring. Even the one potential outcome we do observe may be cut off. As established in Chapter II, we do not observe $T_i$ directly. We observe $\tilde{T}_i = \min(T_i, C_i)$ and the event indicator $\Delta_i = \mathbf{1}\{T_i \leq C_i\}$, where $C_i$ is the censoring time (Voinot et al., 2025, Section 1.1). A customer who cancels at month 14 gives us a clean signal. A customer who is still active at the end of the observation window gives us only the information that their event time exceeds that window. This is the *doubly missing* structure that defines causal survival analysis: the counterfactual we never observe can additionally be right-censored, so we know neither which world they belong to nor how long they survived in it.

### SUTVA in the Time-to-Event Setting

Identification requires the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (Rubin, 1974; Voinot et al., 2025, Assumption 1.1). SUTVA has two components that are equally relevant for time-to-event outcomes.

**No interference.** The potential outcome for individual $i$ depends only on $i$'s own treatment, not on the treatment assigned to any other unit. In a SaaS churn study, one customer's exposure to the new onboarding flow does not affect another customer's churn decision. Interference would arise if the treatment unlocked a collaborative feature requiring a network of treated peers; in that case, $T_i^{(1)}$ would depend on how many neighbors were also treated, violating the assumption.

**Consistency.** The observed event time equals the potential event time under the treatment actually received: $T_i = T_i^{(A_i)}$. This rules out hidden versions of treatment. If "treatment" is a premium tier discount but the discount magnitude varies uncontrolled across users, then $T_i^{(1)}$ is not a single well-defined quantity, and the consistency link breaks.

Together, these conditions are compactly encoded as:

$$T_i = A_i \cdot T_i^{(1)} + (1 - A_i) \cdot T_i^{(0)}$$

### The Causal Estimand: RMST Difference

We need a single number that summarizes the causal effect of treatment on survival. The natural candidate, the hazard ratio, is the wrong choice for causal work, for reasons that are both statistical and conceptual, and that were established formally in Chapter III.

The hazard at time $t$ conditions on surviving up to $t$, a post-treatment variable. Even in a randomized trial, conditioning on this collider opens a backdoor path between treatment and unmeasured risk factors and induces spurious associations (Hernán & Robins, 2020, Fine Point 17.2), the same selection-on-survival mechanism introduced narratively in Chapter I and proven formally in Chapter III. The hazard ratio is also non-collapsible, as established in Chapter III: a marginal hazard ratio cannot be recovered by marginalizing over a conditional one, so heterogeneous populations produce hazard ratios that cannot be meaningfully aggregated (Voinot et al., 2025, Section 1). And under non-proportional hazards, the default in business contexts where treatment effects decay or accumulate over time, the Cox model estimates a weighted average of time-specific ratios with weights that depend on the censoring distribution, producing a quantity that shifts from study to study even when the underlying customer behavior is identical (Uno et al., 2014).

The preferred estimand is the **difference in restricted mean survival time (RMST)**. Define the counterfactual survival functions:

$$S^{(a)}(t) = P\!\left(T_i^{(a)} > t\right), \quad a \in \{0, 1\}$$

Because $T_i^{(a)} \wedge \tau$ is a non-negative random variable, its expectation equals the integral of its survival function (Voinot et al., 2025, Equation 1):

$$E\!\left[T_i^{(a)} \wedge \tau\right] = \int_0^\tau S^{(a)}(t)\, dt$$

The causal estimand is the RMST difference:

$$\tau_{\text{RMST}} = E\!\left[T_i^{(1)} \wedge \tau - T_i^{(0)} \wedge \tau\right]
= \int_0^\tau \!\left[S^{(1)}(t) - S^{(0)}(t)\right] dt$$

This is the area between the two counterfactual survival curves from $0$ to $\tau$, the same quantity introduced as a teaser in Chapter I and now derived from first principles. The interpretation requires no modelling assumptions to communicate: if $\tau_{\text{RMST}} = 2.3$ months with $\tau = 24$ months, the typical treated customer enjoys 2.3 more active months in the first two years than they would have under control. The VP of Data can price that in revenue. The hazard ratio 0.87 requires explaining what a hazard is, why it is not constant, and why the reported figure is a reweighted average of something that was never constant to begin with.

Uno et al. (2014) formalized this argument in a clinical context and the logic transfers directly to product analytics. RMST is collapsible, marginal, and has a direct business interpretation, it is, literally, extra months of customer lifetime gained. The choice of $\tau$ is a design decision, not a statistical one: it should reflect the time horizon over which retention gains are commercially meaningful.

### Identification with Censoring

We have defined the target, $\tau_{\text{RMST}}$, in terms of counterfactual survival curves that are never fully observed. To identify the estimand from observed data, we need assumptions that connect the potential outcomes to the censored variables. Two distinct problems must be addressed: the missing counterfactuals problem and the censoring problem.

**Unconfoundedness** (conditional exchangeability). In observational settings:

$$\{T_i^{(0)},\, T_i^{(1)}\} \perp\!\!\!\perp A_i \mid X_i$$

Conditional on measured covariates $X_i$, treatment assignment is independent of the potential event times. In a SaaS churn context, $X_i$ might include plan tier, usage intensity, firmographics, and prior support interactions. Any factor that simultaneously predicts both treatment receipt and churn, and is absent from $X$, violates this assumption and confounds the estimate.

**Positivity of treatment.** $0 < P(A_i = 1 \mid X_i = x) < 1$ for all $x$ in the support of $X$. Every customer profile must have a positive probability of appearing in both treatment groups.

**Conditional independence of censoring** (Voinot et al., 2025, Assumption 2.5). Right-censoring is a missing data problem: the event time is unobserved above $C_i$. For identification:

$$\{T_i^{(0)},\, T_i^{(1)}\} \perp\!\!\!\perp C_i \mid X_i,\, A_i$$

Within strata defined by covariates and treatment, subjects censored at time $t$ are representative of all subjects still at risk at time $t$. Identification fails when unmeasured factors drive both the event and the censoring. In a SaaS context, the clearest violation is competitive displacement: a customer evaluating a competitor's product will simultaneously reduce platform engagement and become harder to track, they may stop logging in before formally cancelling. Their reduced engagement (unmeasured) predicts both earlier churn and earlier effective censoring. Conditional on observed $X$, their censoring time is no longer independent of their potential event time, and naive survival estimators yield biased causal estimates.

**Positivity of the censoring process** (Voinot et al., 2025, Assumption 2.4). There exists $\varepsilon > 0$ such that $G(\tau \mid A, X) \geq \varepsilon$ almost surely, where $G(t) = P(C \geq t)$. Every subject must have a positive probability of remaining uncensored through the analysis horizon. If entire covariate strata are systematically censored before $\tau$, the survival function is simply not identified in those strata.

One scope limitation is worth stating plainly. The conditional independence assumption above is written in terms of baseline covariates $X_i$, fixed at the start of follow-up. It does not cover settings where censoring is additionally driven by covariates that evolve after treatment begins, a customer downgrading their plan mid-contract before eventually cancelling, say. Handling that properly requires restating the assumption over the full time-varying covariate history and estimating with time-varying weights, the marginal structural model territory of Robins, Hernán & Brumback (2000). Everything in this post, including the IPCW construction in Chapter VI, assumes censoring is adequately described by the baseline covariates already in $X_i$.

Under SUTVA, unconfoundedness, positivity of treatment, conditional independence of censoring, and censoring positivity, the identification chain proceeds as follows (Voinot et al., 2025, Equation 16):

$$\tau_{\text{RMST}} = E\!\left[T(1) \wedge \tau - T(0) \wedge \tau\right]
= E\!\left[E\!\left[T \wedge \tau \mid X, A=1\right] - E\!\left[T \wedge \tau \mid X, A=0\right]\right]$$

Each conditional expectation on the right-hand side involves only observable quantities: $\tilde{T}$, $\Delta$, $A$, and $X$. The missing counterfactuals have been replaced by conditional regressions over the observed data. We have, in principle, a recipe for identification. The estimation problem remains.

### The Fundamental Problem, Visualized

The code below makes the structural missingness explicit. Each row represents a customer. Columns for $T_i(0)$ and $T_i(1)$ expose the fundamental problem directly: for every individual, exactly one potential outcome is observed and the other is `NA`. Some observed outcomes are themselves censored ($\Delta = 0$), meaning even the realized potential outcome is only partially observed. This toy dataset is deliberately small and synthetic; it exists purely to make the missingness pattern visible, not to estimate anything.

  i        = 1:n,
  X        = X,
  A        = A,
  T_tilde  = T_tilde,
  Delta    = Delta,
  `T(0)`   = ifelse(A == 0, T_tilde, NA_real_),  # observed for controls, NA for treated

For treated individuals ($A = 1$), `T(0)` is `NA`, the counterfactual under control is unobservable by design. For controls ($A = 0$), `T(1)` is `NA`. Several rows also have $\Delta = 0$: even the one potential outcome we are permitted to observe is censored, so we only know it exceeds `T_tilde`. This double layer of missingness, counterfactual and censored, is precisely what demands the estimation strategies developed in the chapters that follow.

We now have our target estimand ($\tau_{\text{RMST}} = \int_0^\tau [S^{(1)}(t) - S^{(0)}(t)]\, dt$) and our identification assumptions, but we cannot simply plug them into a formula because the counterfactuals are structurally missing and censoring distorts the empirical distribution of the observed event times. We need an estimation strategy, and the first, most transparent one is g-computation, which models the conditional survival process and then marginalizes over the covariate distribution to recover $\tau_{\text{RMST}}$.

## Chapter V. G-Computation for Survival Outcomes

### From Potential Outcomes to Practical Estimation

In Chapter IV, we established the potential outcomes framework for survival data. We defined the counterfactual survival curves $S^{(1)}(t)$ and $S^{(0)}(t)$, identified the conditions under which they are estimable from observational data, and recognized that the central challenge is confounding: treated and control individuals differ systematically in their baseline characteristics, so naively comparing their Kaplan-Meier curves does not recover a causal effect. We now face the operational question: how do we actually estimate these curves from data? The first and most natural strategy is g-computation, also known as the g-formula or direct standardization, introduced by Robins (1986) in the context of mortality studies with sustained exposures. The idea is elegantly simple: fit a model for the outcome given treatment and confounders, then use that model to simulate "what if" scenarios for the entire population under each treatment level.

### The "What If" Simulation

Suppose we have observed data on $n$ individuals, each with a survival time $T_i$ (possibly censored), a treatment indicator $A_i \in \{0, 1\}$, and a vector of baseline confounders $\mathbf{X}_i$. The g-computation algorithm proceeds in three steps.

First, we fit a survival model for $T$ given $A$ and $\mathbf{X}$ on the observed data. A Cox proportional hazards model is the standard choice, capturing the conditional hazard as $\lambda(t \mid A, \mathbf{X})$. Second, we artificially set $A = 1$ for every individual in the dataset, regardless of their actual treatment assignment, and use the fitted model to predict each person's survival curve $\hat{S}(t \mid A = 1, \mathbf{X} = \mathbf{x}_i)$. We average these individual predictions across the entire sample to obtain the marginal counterfactual curve:

$$\hat{S}^{(1)}(t) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \hat{S}(t \mid A = 1, \mathbf{X} = \mathbf{x}_i)$$

Third, we repeat the procedure with $A = 0$ forced on everyone, yielding $\hat{S}^{(0)}(t)$. These two curves are the g-computation estimates of the causal survival functions under treatment and control, respectively.

The averaging step is the standardization: by marginalizing over the empirical covariate distribution, we recover the population-level counterfactual quantities that were the target of Chapter IV. Intuitively, we are asking: "If everyone in our sample, with all their observed heterogeneity in tenure, usage, and baseline risk, had received the treatment, what would the average survival curve look like?", and then repeating the question for control. This is the statistical equivalent of running a randomized trial in silico, using the rich covariate data we already have to simulate balance across both arms.

A natural question at this point is what happened to censoring, given the weight Chapter IV placed on conditional independence and positivity of the censoring process. The Cox model already accounts for censored observations through its risk-set construction (each subject contributes to the risk set only while under observation and free of the event, exactly the mechanism described for the Kaplan-Meier estimator in Chapter II), so g-computation inherits whatever protection against non-informative censoring the outcome model provides. It does not, however, do anything extra to correct for *informative* censoring. That problem, and the inverse-probability-of-censoring weighting needed to address it directly, is the subject of Chapter VI.

### Why This Solves the Confounding Problem

Confounding arises because the distribution of $\mathbf{X}$ differs between the treated and control groups in observational data. A naive comparison of Kaplan-Meier curves by treatment arm conflates the effect of treatment with the effect of the confounders associated with treatment assignment. G-computation breaks this association by construction. When we predict outcomes under $A = 1$ for every individual, we are constructing a hypothetical world in which treatment is assigned universally and is therefore statistically independent of $\mathbf{X}$. The same holds when we predict under $A = 0$. By comparing the two hypothetical worlds rather than the two observed subpopulations, we isolate the causal effect of treatment from the confounding structure, provided the outcome model is right; the next section returns to what happens when it is not. This is the standardization principle applied to the causal inference setting: the marginal curves are weighted averages of the conditional curves, where the weights reflect the full population distribution of confounders rather than the treatment-conditional distributions.

The practical implication for decision-makers is immediate. Instead of reporting a hazard ratio of 0.8, which is difficult to interpret and prone to misunderstanding, you can now say: "If we roll out this intervention to all customers, we expect their average active lifetime to increase by 2.3 months, adjusted for their initial engagement levels." G-computation converts an opaque relative measure into an actionable absolute one, grounded in the actual covariate distribution of the target population.

### The `adjustedCurves` Package

Implementing g-computation for survival outcomes from scratch is deceptively complex. One must correctly extract baseline hazard estimates from the Cox model, handle tied event times, manage censoring in the counterfactual predictions, and construct valid confidence intervals that propagate uncertainty through the entire standardization procedure. The `adjustedCurves` package (Denz, 2024) provides a unified, battle-tested interface that abstracts away these complexities.

The package wraps g-computation, IPTW, AIPTW, matching, and pseudo-value approaches behind a single consistent function call. For g-computation specifically, it handles Cox model predictions correctly, applies standardization over the observed covariate distribution, and constructs confidence intervals via nonparametric bootstrapping, the recommended approach for variance estimation in this setting, given the complexity of analytical standard error formulas (Voinot et al., 2025, Section 3). Unlike older manual workflows that required piecing together multiple packages and custom bootstrap loops, `adjustedCurves` ensures that estimated survival curves satisfy the monotonicity and boundedness constraints automatically guaranteed by using a Cox outcome model.

The package was validated in the simulation study of Denz, Klaaßen-Mielke, and Timmesfeld (2023), which compared ten adjustment methods across a range of sample sizes and model specification scenarios. G-computation was the most efficient estimator (lowest standard error) when the outcome model was correctly specified, particularly when the confounders were strong predictors of survival. It outperformed both IPTW-based approaches and propensity score matching on variance grounds. The same study confirmed, however, that g-computation is not doubly robust: if the Cox model is misspecified, the counterfactual predictions are systematically biased, and no amount of bootstrapping can rescue them.

### Estimating the Causal Effect: Adjusted RMST

Once we have the adjusted survival curves $\hat{S}^{(1)}(t)$ and $\hat{S}^{(0)}(t)$, summarizing the causal effect uses the same RMST machinery derived in Chapter IV. The restricted mean survival time up to a pre-specified time horizon $\tau$ is the area under the counterfactual survival curve, $\text{RMST}^{(a)}(\tau) = \int_0^{\tau} S^{(a)}(t) \, dt$, and the causal RMST difference is the g-computation plug-in estimate of $\tau_{\text{RMST}}$:

$$\hat\tau_{\text{RMST}}(\tau) = \widehat{\text{RMST}}^{(1)}(\tau) - \widehat{\text{RMST}}^{(0)}(\tau) = \int_0^{\tau} \bigl[ \hat{S}^{(1)}(t) - \hat{S}^{(0)}(t) \bigr] \, dt$$

This quantity has a direct interpretation: it is the expected additional survival time up to horizon $\tau$ that the population would accrue under treatment versus control. The horizon $\tau$ should be chosen on business or clinical grounds, not by data-driven optimization, exactly as discussed in the callout box in Chapter II. In a customer retention context, for instance, $\tau = 12$ months might correspond to the annual planning cycle, and $\hat\tau_{\text{RMST}}(12)$ would quantify the expected gain in active customer-months from the intervention. The `adjustedCurves` package computes this automatically from the estimated curves via `adjusted_rmst()`, with bootstrap confidence intervals that properly propagate uncertainty from the outcome model and the censoring process alike.

### Implementation in R

The following code demonstrates the full workflow: generating a reproducible synthetic dataset, fitting a Cox model, estimating the confounder-adjusted survival curves via g-computation, visualizing them, and extracting the RMST difference. As with the toy examples in earlier chapters, this dataset is synthetic and chosen purely to illustrate the mechanics; Chapter VIII applies the identical workflow to a real customer dataset.

```{r}
#| label: g-comp-survival
#| message: false

#| warning: false

library(survival)
library(adjustedCurves)

# -----------------------------------------------------------------
# Synthetic dataset: 500 individuals with right-censored survival
# times, a binary treatment, and two baseline confounders.
# Replace `data` with your own dataset in practice.
# Variables required:
#   time       : observed event or censoring time (numeric)
#   status     : event indicator (1 = event, 0 = censored)
#   treatment  : binary treatment assignment (0 or 1)
#   covariate1 : continuous baseline covariate (e.g., tenure in months)
#   covariate2 : binary baseline covariate (e.g., premium-tier flag)
# -----------------------------------------------------------------
set.seed(42)
n <- 500
data <- data.frame(
  treatment  = rbinom(n, 1, 0.5),
  covariate1 = rnorm(n, 55, 10),
  covariate2 = rbinom(n, 1, 0.3)
)

# Latent event time, never observed directly once censoring is applied.
event_time <- rexp(n, rate = 0.05 + 0.02 * data$treatment +
                          0.003 * data$covariate1 +
                          0.01  * data$covariate2)

# Administrative censoring: nobody is followed past max_followup time
# units, so the observed support is bounded and known in advance. This
# is what makes a fixed tau safe to choose below, rather than guessing
# at a horizon that might exceed whatever time happens to come out of
# the random draw above.
max_followup    <- 24
censor_time     <- runif(n, min = 0, max = max_followup)
data$time       <- pmin(event_time, censor_time)
data$status     <- as.integer(event_time <= censor_time)

# adjustedCurves requires the grouping variable to be a factor,
# not a bare 0/1 integer, both for coxph() and for adjustedsurv().
data$treatment <- factor(data$treatment, levels = c(0, 1))

# -----------------------------------------------------------------
# Step 1: Fit a Cox proportional hazards model on the observed data.
# Include treatment AND all confounders as predictors.
# x = TRUE is required by adjustedCurves for individual-level
# predictions at the standardization step.
# -----------------------------------------------------------------
cox_fit <- coxph(
  Surv(time, status) ~ treatment + covariate1 + covariate2,
  data = data,
  x    = TRUE
)

# -----------------------------------------------------------------
# Step 2: G-computation via adjustedsurv().
# method = "direct" invokes the g-formula (direct standardization):
#   the function sets treatment to each level for ALL individuals,
#   predicts individual survival curves, and averages them.
# conf_int = TRUE + bootstrap = TRUE: nonparametric bootstrap CIs
#   (n_boot = 500 is a reasonable default; use >= 1000 for
#   publication-quality estimates).
# -----------------------------------------------------------------
adj_curves <- adjustedsurv(
  data          = data,
  variable      = "treatment",
  ev_time       = "time",
  event         = "status",
  method        = "direct",
  outcome_model = cox_fit,
  conf_int      = TRUE,
  bootstrap     = TRUE,
  n_boot        = 10
)

# -----------------------------------------------------------------
# Step 3: Plot S^1(t) and S^0(t) with pointwise 95% CIs.
# -----------------------------------------------------------------
plot(adj_curves,
     conf_int    = TRUE,
     xlab        = "Time",
     ylab        = "Adjusted Survival Probability",
     legend.title = "Treatment")

# -----------------------------------------------------------------
# Step 4: Causal RMST difference up to horizon tau.
# adjusted_rmst() requires `to` to fall within the latest time at
# which the adjusted curve is actually evaluated. For method =
# "direct", the package documentation states that the adjusted
# survival probability is estimated only at observed EVENT times in
# the pooled sample, not at every observed time (censored
# observations do not add evaluation points). tau is therefore set
# safely below the latest observed EVENT time, not the latest
# observed time overall, which can be a censored observation and
# would otherwise still trigger the "'to' cannot be greater than the
# latest observed time" error even after bounding by max_followup.
# The result gives Delta_RMST and its 95% CI in the same units
# as `time`.
# -----------------------------------------------------------------
latest_event_time <- max(data$time[data$status == 1])
tau <- floor(0.9 * latest_event_time)

rmst_diff <- adjusted_rmst(
  adj_curves,
  to       = tau,
  contrast = "diff",
  conf_int = TRUE
)

print(rmst_diff)
```

The `adjustedsurv()` function encapsulates the entire g-computation logic: it extracts the fitted Cox model, sets the treatment variable to each level for all individuals, predicts and averages the individual survival curves, and bootstraps the full procedure for confidence intervals. The `adjusted_rmst()` function then integrates the resulting curves to produce the causal RMST estimate and its uncertainty, with no additional manual computation required.

### The Achilles Heel: Model Dependence

G-computation is only as good as the outcome model that underlies it. The estimator is consistent, under correct specification, and highly efficient, but this efficiency comes at a price. If the Cox model is misspecified, perhaps because a nonlinear covariate effect is modeled as linear, or because an important interaction is omitted, then the predictions $\hat{S}(t \mid A = z, \mathbf{X}_i)$ are systematically wrong. When we average biased predictions over the covariate distribution, we obtain biased counterfactual curves. There is no self-correcting mechanism: g-computation places all its assumptions in the outcome model, and a wrong model produces a wrong answer regardless of sample size.

Denz et al. (2023) confirm this empirically: in scenarios with misspecified outcome models, the g-formula can exhibit persistent bias that does not shrink as $n$ grows. Voinot et al. (2025) further show that the choice of modelling strategy matters, with single pooled models ("S-learners") being particularly sensitive to proportional hazards violations compared to arm-specific models ("T-learners"). In practice, before committing to g-computation, you should check the proportional hazards assumption, assess whether functional forms are appropriate, and include all strong predictors of the outcome, not just the confounders, since omitting a prognostic variable can induce residual confounding through unobserved heterogeneity even after standardization.

G-computation gives us a powerful and efficient first tool. In well-specified settings, it outperforms every alternative reviewed in Denz et al. (2023) on variance grounds. But its reliance on a single correctly specified outcome model is a genuine vulnerability. Chapter VI confronts this directly: we turn to inverse probability weighting (IPW), which places its identifying assumptions in a treatment assignment model rather than an outcome model, and then to augmented inverse probability weighting (AIPW), which combines both model types to achieve double robustness, remaining consistent even if one of the two models is wrong.

## Chapter VI. IPW and AIPW: When the Outcome Model Is Wrong

G-computation, as we saw in Chapter V, is elegant: fit an outcome model, predict counterfactual survival under each treatment, and average over the covariate distribution. But elegance is not robustness. If the Cox model is misspecified, a critical interaction omitted, a non-linearity ignored, the proportional hazards assumption silently violated, the counterfactual predictions are systematically biased, and no amount of additional data corrects the error. That bias is structural, not statistical: it does not shrink as the sample grows. If your data science team builds a flawed Cox model, g-computation will give the CEO the wrong answer, and not a small or self-correcting kind of wrong.

This chapter introduces two methods that escape that trap: inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) and its augmented extension, AIPW. We will see that survival data introduces a complication absent from the cross-sectional literature, informative censoring forces us to construct *two* sets of weights, not one, and that AIPW resolves nearly all of these concerns by combining the strengths of both approaches into a single, doubly robust estimator.

### Inverse Probability of Treatment Weighting

IPTW takes a fundamentally different approach from g-computation. Instead of modelling the outcome, it models the *treatment assignment mechanism*. The intuition is geometric: in an observational study, treated and untreated customers differ systematically on their covariates. IPTW removes this imbalance by reweighting each observation by the inverse of its probability of receiving the treatment it actually received, constructing a pseudo-population where treatment assignment is independent of confounders (Robins, Hernán & Brumback, 2000).

Formally, let $e(X_i) = P(A_i = 1 \mid X_i)$ denote the propensity score, estimated via logistic regression or more flexible machine learning methods. The IPTW weight for individual $i$ is:

$$w_i^{\text{IPTW}} = \frac{A_i}{e(X_i)} + \frac{1 - A_i}{1 - e(X_i)}.$$

In the pseudo-population induced by these weights, the distribution of covariates is balanced across treatment arms, and a simple weighted comparison of survival curves recovers the marginal counterfactual survival curves $S^{(a)}(t)$. Applied to survival data, the weights enter a weighted Kaplan-Meier estimator:

$$\hat{S}_{\text{IPTW}}(t \mid A = a) = \prod_{t_k \leq t} \left(1 - \frac{\sum_i w_i \cdot \mathbf{1}(\tilde{T}_i = t_k,\, \Delta_i = 1,\, A_i = a)}{\sum_i w_i \cdot \mathbf{1}(\tilde{T}_i \geq t_k,\, A_i = a)}\right),$$

first formalized by Cole & Hernán (2004) and Xie & Liu (2005). No outcome model is needed. This is the logic underlying marginal structural models (Robins, Hernán & Brumback, 2000), which provide a principled framework for causal inference whenever the treatment mechanism can be modelled, including, importantly, settings with time-varying confounding where standard regression adjustment fails entirely.

### The Survival Twist: Inverse Probability of Censoring Weighting

Here is where survival analysis diverges from the cross-sectional IPW literature, and where practitioners most commonly go wrong. In addition to the selection bias introduced by non-random treatment assignment, survival data suffer from a second form of selection bias: informative censoring.

Consider a subscription business measuring time to customer churn. Highly engaged users are both less likely to churn (the event) and less likely to be lost to follow-up (censored). The observed, uncensored sample therefore overrepresents engaged users at later time points, making survival look artificially good. Standard IPTW corrects for treatment confounding but leaves this censoring bias entirely intact. Voinot et al. (2025) document exactly this pattern in their simulation study: unadjusted estimators exhibit substantial, persistent bias whenever the censoring mechanism depends on covariates.

The solution is inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW). Let $G(t \mid X_i, A_i) = P(C_i \geq t \mid X_i, A_i)$ denote the conditional survival function of the censoring time. The IPCW transformation replaces each observed time with a reweighted version:

$$T_i^{\text{IPCW}} = \frac{\mathbf{1}(\Delta_i = 1) \cdot (\tilde{T}_i \wedge \tau)}{G(\tilde{T}_i \wedge \tau \mid X_i, A_i)},$$

upweighting uncensored individuals who were at high risk of censoring, so they represent the censored subjects they resemble. Individuals who are censored receive weight zero at all times after censoring.

In observational survival analysis under informative censoring, both weight sets are required simultaneously. The combined weight for individual $i$ at time $t$ is:

$$W_i(t) = \frac{1}{P(A_i \mid X_i)} \times \frac{1}{G(t \mid X_i, A_i)}.$$

The first factor balances confounding; the second corrects for informative censoring. This product creates a pseudo-population balanced on both the treatment assignment and censoring mechanisms. Both must be correctly specified for the IPTW-IPCW estimator to be consistent, which brings us directly to AIPW.

### A Practical Warning: Stabilized Weights

Before augmentation, a necessary digression on weight pathology. Raw IPW weights can be extreme. A customer with $e(X_i) = 0.02$ who happens to receive treatment gets a weight of 50. A handful of such observations dominate the weighted estimate, inflating variance to unacceptable levels and rendering confidence intervals almost meaningless. Denz et al. (2023) demonstrate this empirically: IPTW methods consistently show higher variance than g-computation, with the gap most pronounced in small samples and when propensity scores concentrate near the boundary.

The standard remedy is stabilized weights (Robins, Hernán & Brumback, 2000). Replace the numerator 1 with the marginal probability of the observed treatment:

$$sw_i = \frac{P(A_i = a_i)}{P(A_i = a_i \mid X_i = x_i)}.$$

Stabilized weights cluster around 1 rather than exhibiting the long right tail of unstabilized weights, yielding the same consistent estimates with substantially lower variance. The same stabilization applies to the censoring weights, replacing 1 with $P(C_i > t)$ in the numerator. Always use stabilized weights in practice; the efficiency gain is essentially free.

### Augmented IPW and Double Robustness

IPTW resolves g-computation's single-model dependence, but creates a symmetric vulnerability: all causal conclusions now flow through the propensity and censoring models. Misspecify those, and the estimator fails. We have traded one fragility for another.

Augmented inverse probability weighting (AIPW), referred to as AIPTW in the survival literature, resolves this by combining *both* modelling strategies into a single estimating equation. The construction, rooted in the semiparametric efficiency theory of Scharfstein, Rotnitzky & Robins (1999) and implemented for adjusted survival curves by Denz et al. (2023), augments the IPTW estimator with a correction term derived from the outcome model:

<!-- EDITOR'S NOTE: The original draft of this formula used 1(T_i > t), referencing the latent
true event time T_i. T_i is unobservable past censoring by construction (Chapter II), so no
estimator, doubly robust or otherwise, can actually condition on it. Corrected to the observed time
T-tilde_i = min(T_i, C_i), consistent with every other estimator formula in this post. -->

$$\hat{S}_z^{\text{AIPW}}(t) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left[ W_i(t) \cdot \mathbf{1}(\tilde{T}_i > t) + \bigl(1 - W_i(t)\bigr) \cdot \hat{S}(t \mid A_i = z, X_i) \right].$$

The term $\hat{S}(t \mid A_i = z, X_i)$ is the Cox model prediction, the same g-computation plug-in from Chapter V. When the weighting models are wrong, the outcome model term corrects the bias. When the outcome model is wrong, the weighting term corrects the bias. In simplified terms: AIPW is the IPTW estimator with g-computation predictions added back in as an augmentation, and each component acts as insurance for the other.

The formal property this construction delivers is **double robustness**, defined precisely as follows. The AIPW estimator $\hat{S}_z^{\text{AIPW}}(t)$ is consistent for the true counterfactual survival curve $S_z(t)$ if *either*:

- the outcome model $E[Y \mid A, X]$, equivalently, the conditional survival function $S(t \mid A, X)$, is correctly specified, *or*
- the propensity score model $P(A \mid X)$ *and* the censoring survival model $P(C \mid X, A)$ are both correctly specified,

even if the other set of models is misspecified. This is the guarantee Scharfstein, Rotnitzky & Robins (1999) established in the semiparametric efficiency framework: when *both* sets of models are correct, the AIPW estimator achieves the semiparametric efficiency bound, making it the most efficient estimator in its class. When only one is correct, it remains consistent, a property no single-model method can claim.

The practical implications are direct. If your data science team correctly models who receives treatment and which customers are censored but the Cox outcome model is misspecified, the AIPW estimate is still valid. Conversely, if the propensity and censoring models are wrong but the Cox model captures the true hazard, AIPW remains unbiased. The simulation evidence confirms this: across all five misspecification scenarios in Denz et al. (2023), AIPTW-based estimators remain unbiased whenever at least one model component is correct, while g-computation fails systematically the moment the outcome model is wrong. Voinot et al. (2025) extend this finding under informative censoring, showing that AIPW achieves the lowest mean squared error across a wide range of realistic scenarios, outperforming IPTW in finite samples, particularly when propensity scores are weak or censoring rates are high.

### R Code: Adjusted Curves with IPW and AIPW

The `adjustedCurves` package (Denz et al., 2023) implements both methods through a unified interface. The central function is `adjustedsurv()`, dispatching to the appropriate estimator via the `method` argument. For IPTW, only the treatment model is required. For AIPW, all three models, outcome, treatment, and censoring, must be supplied; double robustness holds as long as the outcome model *or* the pair of propensity and censoring models is correctly specified.

```{r}
#| eval: false
library(survival)
library(adjustedCurves)

# ── 0. Generate sim_data ──────────────────────────────────────────────────
# A synthetic dataset with informative censoring: the censoring hazard
# depends on the same covariates that drive the event hazard, exactly the
# scenario this chapter argues standard IPTW alone cannot fix. treatment
# is stored as a factor, since adjustedsurv() requires a factor grouping
# variable (see the editor's note on this requirement in Chapter V).
set.seed(123)
n <- 1000

age       <- rnorm(n, mean = 50, sd = 12)
severity  <- rnorm(n, mean = 0, sd = 1)
biomarker <- rbinom(n, 1, 0.4)
treatment <- rbinom(n, 1, 0.5)

# Event time: hazard increases with age, severity, and biomarker presence;
# treatment reduces the hazard.
event_time <- rexp(
  n,
  rate = 0.04 + 0.10 * (severity > 0) + 0.05 * biomarker +
         0.002 * age - 0.02 * treatment
)

# Censoring time: deliberately informative, engaged (low-severity) patients
# are less likely to be censored early, mirroring the competitive-
# displacement mechanism described in Chapter IV.
censor_time <- rexp(n, rate = 0.03 + 0.04 * (severity > 0))

time  <- pmin(event_time, censor_time)
event <- as.integer(event_time <= censor_time)

sim_data <- data.frame(
  time      = time,
  event     = event,
  treatment = factor(treatment, levels = c(0, 1)),
  age       = age,
  severity  = severity,
  biomarker = biomarker
)

# ── 1. Fit the propensity score model ─────────────────────────────────────────
# Models P(A = 1 | X): the treatment assignment mechanism.
# One ingredient of double robustness; logistic regression is standard.
ps_model <- glm(
  treatment ~ age + severity + biomarker,
  data   = sim_data,
  family = binomial
)

# ── 2. Fit the outcome model (Cox) ────────────────────────────────────────────
# Models S(t | A, X): the conditional survival function.
# Required for AIPW. Misspecification here is protected against
# when the propensity + censoring models are correct.
cox_model <- coxph(
  Surv(time, event) ~ treatment + age + severity + biomarker,
  data = sim_data,
  x    = TRUE          # retain design matrix for prediction
)

# ── 3. Fit the censoring model ────────────────────────────────────────────────
# Models P(C > t | X, A): survival function of the censoring time.
# Needed whenever censoring depends on covariates (informative censoring).
# The event indicator is flipped: censoring is now the "event".
cens_model <- coxph(
  Surv(time, event == 0) ~ age + severity + biomarker,
  data = sim_data,
  x    = TRUE
)

# ── 4. IPTW-adjusted survival curves ──────────────────────────────────────────
# method = "iptw_km" (the IPTW Kaplan-Meier estimator of Xie & Liu (2005))
# weights observations by the inverse probability of the treatment they
# actually received, using the propensity score model only. No outcome
# model is required.
#
# A note on censoring: the adjustedCurves package does not let "iptw_km"
# take a separate censoring_model argument the way "aiptw" does below.
# Per the package documentation, the only way to fold in inverse
# probability of censoring weights here would be to multiply them into a
# single combined weight vector and pass that vector directly to
# treatment_model. This estimator therefore corrects for treatment
# confounding only, not for informative censoring, which is precisely
# why it is not the estimator of choice once dropout is suspected to
# depend on covariates, and precisely the gap AIPW closes next.
adj_ipw <- adjustedsurv(
  data            = sim_data,
  variable        = "treatment",
  ev_time         = "time",
  event           = "event",
  method          = "iptw_km",
  conf_int        = TRUE,
  treatment_model = ps_model       # P(A | X)
)

# ── 5. AIPW-adjusted survival curves (doubly robust) ─────────────────────────
# method = "aiptw" combines all three models into one estimating equation.
# Consistent if EITHER the Cox outcome model OR the propensity + censoring
# models are correctly specified, not necessarily both.
adj_aipw <- adjustedsurv(
  data            = sim_data,
  variable        = "treatment",
  ev_time         = "time",
  event           = "event",
  method          = "aiptw",
  conf_int        = TRUE,
  outcome_model   = cox_model,     # S(t | A, X)
  treatment_model = ps_model,      # P(A | X)
  censoring_model = cens_model     # P(C | X, A)
)

# ── 6. Plot adjusted survival curves ──────────────────────────────────────────
par(mfrow = c(1, 2))
plot(adj_ipw,  title = "IPTW-Adjusted Curves",
     xlab = "Time", ylab = "Survival Probability")
plot(adj_aipw, title = "AIPW-Adjusted Curves",
     xlab = "Time", ylab = "Survival Probability")

# ── 7. Restricted Mean Survival Time (RMST) ───────────────────────────────────
# RMST difference = area between the two counterfactual survival curves
# up to horizon tau. Interpretable: average months gained (or lost)
# attributable to treatment over a fixed follow-up window.
# As in Chapter V, `to` must fall within the latest observed EVENT time
# (method = "iptw_km" and "aiptw" both evaluate the adjusted curve only at
# observed event times), so tau is computed from the data rather than
# hard-coded.
latest_event_time <- max(sim_data$time[sim_data$event == 1])
tau <- floor(0.9 * latest_event_time)

rmst_ipw  <- adjusted_rmst(adj_ipw,  to = tau)
rmst_aipw <- adjusted_rmst(adj_aipw, to = tau)

print(rmst_ipw)
print(rmst_aipw)
```

A few implementation notes worth internalizing. First, always verify the weight distribution after fitting: `print(adj_ipw)` reports summary statistics for the estimated weights, and values consistently above 10-15 signal positivity violations that should be investigated before interpreting the results. Second, for AIPW, supply the censoring model whenever your study involves informative dropout; leaving `censoring_model = NULL` is equivalent to assuming $P(C \mid X, A) = P(C)$, an assumption routinely violated in observational databases. The plain IPTW-KM estimator above has no equivalent argument to set in this package, which is exactly why it cannot be trusted on its own once dropout is suspected to depend on covariates. Third, for AIPW, use the same covariate sets across the three models where possible; asymmetric specifications can undermine the efficiency of the augmentation term even when both model pairs are technically correct.

## Chapter VII. Heterogeneous Effects: Who Benefits More? {#sec-heterogeneous-effects}

Every method we have used so far, from g-computation to AIPW, has been built to answer one number: the population average treatment effect. That number tells a CFO whether an intervention works. It tells almost nothing about how to allocate next quarter's budget.

### The Tyranny of the Average

Suppose a retention campaign produces an average RMST gain of 1.5 months over a 24-month horizon. A reasonable executive reads that as "the campaign buys 1.5 months of extra tenure per user, on average." The average, however, is compatible with wildly different underlying realities. It is consistent with a world where every user gains roughly 1.5 months. It is just as consistent with a world where high-risk users (those flagged by a churn score in the top decile, say) gain 4 months, while low-risk users gain nothing at all.

If you only ever estimate the ATE, you cannot tell these two worlds apart, and that distinction is exactly the information a targeting strategy needs. Rolling the campaign out to the entire base is efficient in the first scenario. Doing the same in the second scenario means paying full price to move a number that, for most users, the intervention never actually touches. The conditional average treatment effect (CATE), written $\tau(x)$, exists to recover that lost resolution.

<!-- EDITOR'S NOTE: tau(x) here is the conditional treatment effect function (a function of
covariates x, estimated from data), introduced in Chapter IV's editor's note as a deliberate
notational collision with tau (no argument), the truncation horizon used as a fixed analyst choice
throughout Chapters I, II, IV, V, VI, and VIII. The two are unrelated quantities that happen to
share the Greek letter for reasons of established literature convention (grf and the causal
survival forest papers both use tau(x) for the CATE). Read tau as "the time horizon you chose" and
tau(x) as "the estimated effect for a customer with covariates x." -->

### From Causal Forests to Causal Survival Forests

The natural tool for estimating $\tau(x)$ nonparametrically is the causal forest of Athey, Tibshirani, and Wager (2019), implemented in `grf` as `causal_forest()`. A causal forest grows trees that split on covariates likely to drive treatment effect heterogeneity, then uses the resulting adaptive neighborhoods to run a localized version of Robinson's residual-on-residual regression at every point $x$.

That machinery, as written, needs a fully observed outcome for every unit. In survival data we rarely have one. We only see $U_i = T_i \wedge C_i$ and the event indicator $\Delta_i$, and a meaningful fraction of users are still active (censored) at the time we pull the data.

::: {.callout-warning}
**A naive workaround here is the single most dangerous mistake in this chapter.** Feeding `causal_forest()` the censored time $U_i$ directly, or restricting the sample to completed cases only ($\Delta_i = 1$), does not produce a conservative or roughly right CATE. It produces a biased one. Censored users are, by construction, the ones who survived at least until $U_i$, and treating $U_i$ as their final outcome systematically understates their true survival time. If censoring rates differ across treatment arms or across regions of covariate space (which they almost always do in observational settings), this bias varies with $x$ and contaminates exactly the heterogeneity signal you are trying to recover.
:::

Cui, Kosorok, Sverdrup, Wager, and Zhu (2023) solve this with `causal_survival_forest()`, available in the `grf` package (Tibshirani et al., 2024). The function keeps the generalized random forest scaffolding (adaptive splitting, honesty, the bootstrap of little bags for confidence intervals) but replaces the splitting criterion with one built from doubly robust, Neyman-orthogonal estimating equations adapted from the semiparametric survival literature, with the full construction and asymptotic theory developed in Sverdrup & Wager (2024). In practice, every observation, censored or not, contributes to every candidate split through a pseudo-outcome that combines a weight derived from the estimated conditional survival function of the censoring process with an imputation term for what each censored user would likely have done. A simpler variant, also available in `grf`, reweights complete cases only using inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW); it is easier to reason about, but it throws away the partial information a censored observation still carries (this user had not churned by month nine, for instance) and it is not robust to errors in the censoring model. Log-rank splitting, by contrast, is a feature of classical random survival forests (Ishwaran, Kogalur, Blackstone & Lauer, 2008), not of causal survival forests, which target the treatment effect directly rather than the survival distribution itself. The doubly robust default is the one you should reach for unless you have a specific reason not to.

One caveat carries over directly from the population-level estimators in Chapters V and VI: a causal survival forest still needs covariate overlap between treatment arms to do its job. In a region of $X$ where one arm has little or no support, the forest cannot estimate $\hat\tau(x)$ from comparable neighbors, it extrapolates from whatever nearby observations exist, and the result can look like confident, well-defined heterogeneity while actually reflecting the adaptive tree structure more than the underlying data. The propensity overlap check from Chapter IX applies here too, just evaluated locally rather than only on average across the whole sample.

### Defining the Conditional RMST

Formally, for a chosen horizon $h$, the estimand is the conditional restricted mean survival time difference:

$$
\tau(x) = E\big[T^{(1)} - T^{(0)} \mid X = x\big]
$$

or, in its truncated and identifiable form,

$$
\tau^h(x) = E\big[T^{(1)} \wedge h \mid X = x\big] - E\big[T^{(0)} \wedge h \mid X = x\big].
$$

This is the same RMST contrast established in Chapters I and IV, now indexed by $x$ instead of averaged over the whole population. Each leaf of the forest behaves like a localized, doubly robust matched comparison: the adaptive weights $\alpha_i(x)$ that `grf` computes internally tell you which training observations the forest considers comparable to a target user $x$, and $\hat\tau(x)$ is built from that weighted neighborhood using the same orthogonal score that protects the global ATE against censoring and confounding. The horizon $h$ is not a free statistical convenience either. Pick it by looking at where censoring starts to dominate the data, the same way you would choose a truncation point for a population-level RMST in Chapter II's terms, since behavior past that point is not identified from the sample in front of you.

### Variable Importance and Targeting

A CATE forest does more than produce a number per user. Calling `variable_importance()` returns, for every covariate, a depth-weighted count of how often the forest split on it. High importance means the forest found that variable useful for separating users into groups with different treatment effects, which is a direct, data-driven map of where the heterogeneity actually lives.

This is where the chapter earns its keep on a P&L. Suppose the retention offer costs €50 per user (CRM outreach, agent time, the discount itself), and each extra month of retained tenure is worth €15 in margin. The break-even CATE is then €50 divided by €15, or about 3.3 months: below that gain, the offer destroys value, above it, the offer pays for itself. If variable importance points to `tenure` and `weekly_logins` as the dominant drivers of $\hat\tau(x)$, the targeting rule writes itself: deploy the offer only to users with `tenure < 3 months` and low `weekly_logins`, where $\hat\tau(x)$ clears the 3.3-month payback threshold. We cannot afford to target everyone at €50 a head, and the CATE is precisely the number that tells us where the spend is justified. Users outside that region get pulled from the rollout entirely, not because they don't matter, but because the forest has told you, with a doubly robust guarantee, that the campaign does not move their survival curve enough to clear the bar.

### Fitting a Causal Survival Forest in R

```{r}
library(grf)

# ---- Reproducible synthetic churn data ----
set.seed(2026)
n <- 4000
X <- cbind(
  tenure        = round(runif(n, 0, 36)),
  weekly_logins = round(rpois(n, 4)),
  monthly_spend = round(rlnorm(n, log(40), 0.4), 1)
)
W <- rbinom(n, 1, 0.5)  # randomized retention offer

# Ground truth: the offer only meaningfully helps short-tenure,
# low-engagement users, exactly the segment we expect to target.
benefits  <- (X[, "tenure"] < 6) & (X[, "weekly_logins"] < 3)
base_rate <- 0.07
T_event   <- rexp(n, rate = base_rate / (1 + 1.5 * benefits * W))
C         <- rep(24, n)              # administrative censoring at 24 months
Y         <- pmin(T_event, C)
D         <- as.integer(T_event <= C)

train_idx <- sample(seq_len(n), 0.7 * n)
X_train <- X[train_idx, ];  Y_train <- Y[train_idx]
W_train <- W[train_idx];    D_train <- D[train_idx]
X_test  <- X[-train_idx, ]

# ---- Fit the causal survival forest ----
csf <- causal_survival_forest(
  X         = X_train,
  Y         = Y_train,
  W         = W_train,
  D         = D_train,
  W.hat     = 0.5,        # known randomization probability
  horizon   = 24,
  num.trees = 4000
)

# Doubly robust ATE, as a sanity check against the population average
average_treatment_effect(csf)

# Predict the conditional RMST gain for every user in the held-out test set
tau_hat <- predict(csf, newdata = X_test)$predictions

# Which covariates drive the heterogeneity?
var_imp <- variable_importance(csf)
ranking <- data.frame(covariate = colnames(X_train), importance = var_imp)
print(ranking[order(-ranking$importance), ])

# Visualize the spread of CATEs to spot the targetable segment
hist(tau_hat, breaks = 40,
     main = "Distribution of estimated CATEs (RMST gain, months)",
     xlab = expression(hat(tau)(x)))
```

The histogram of `tau_hat` is usually the most persuasive slide you can bring to a targeting conversation. It shows, in one picture, exactly how much of that 1.5-month average is concentrated in a narrow, identifiable, and addressable segment, rather than spread evenly across the base. Before you commit budget to that segment, it is worth asking whether the heterogeneity is real or just forest noise; `grf`'s `rank_average_treatment_effect()` answers exactly that question by comparing the ATE among the top-prioritized users to the overall ATE, and a 95% confidence interval for the area under that curve that excludes zero is good evidence the targeting rule is doing real work. To use it without overstating precision, evaluate it on a sample the CATE forest did not see, fitting a separate forest on the test fold for scoring, rather than reusing `csf`'s own training data twice.

## Chapter VIII. Full R Implementation: Customer Churn Case Study

Every dataset used so far in this post, the `lung` cohort in Chapter II, the simulated `surv_df` in Chapter III, the eight-row toy `df` in Chapter IV, the synthetic `data` in Chapter V, the simulated `sim_data` in Chapter VI, and the synthetic churn population in Chapter VII, was chosen to teach exactly one mechanical point as cleanly as possible, with no distractions from messy real-world covariates. Everything in Chapters II through VII has been building toward this moment. We have the Kaplan-Meier estimator, the log-rank test, the Cox model, and the potential-outcomes machinery for survival data. Now we put it all to work on a problem every subscription business recognizes: does offering a retention service actually keep customers around longer, or are we looking at a correlation that happens to wear a causal disguise? The VP of Data does not want a p-value on her desk. She wants a euro figure, and this chapter delivers one, showing exactly how naive and causal survival analysis can disagree by a wide margin.

### Data Setup

We use the IBM Telco Customer Churn dataset (IBM, n.d.), a standard benchmark for subscription analytics with 7,043 customers. Our time variable is `tenure`, measured in months. The event is `churn` (recoded from "Yes"/"No" to 1/0). The intervention of interest is `tech_support`, a retention add-on that a customer either has or does not have. Covariates include `contract` (month-to-month, one-year, two-year), `monthly_charges`, `phone_service`, and `internet_service`. Tenure in this dataset tops out at 72 months, but the restriction horizon $\tau$ is set just below the latest observed churn event rather than at 72 itself, since the adjusted curves used later in this chapter are only evaluated up to the last point at which an event actually occurred, not the last point at which any customer, churned or still active, was observed. Fixing $\tau$ at the dataset's nominal ceiling rather than at its actual evaluable support is a common source of silent extrapolation errors, and is exactly the kind of detail Chapter IX's diagnostic checklist asks you to verify before trusting a number.

```{r}
library(survival)
library(adjustedCurves)
library(dplyr)

# ── Step 1: Naive view (Kaplan-Meier and log-rank) ────────────────────────

print(km_naive, rmean = tau)

logrank_naive <- survdiff(Surv(tenure, event) ~ tech_support, data = churn_data)

# ── Step 2: Associational view (standard Cox) ─────────────────────────────
cox_fit <- coxph(

  data = churn_data,

summary(cox_fit)

The `Surv()` object binds time and event into a single structure that every downstream function in the `survival` and `adjustedCurves` ecosystems knows how to consume. Getting this object right is the foundation of everything that follows.

### Step 1: The Naive View (Kaplan-Meier and Log-Rank)

Our first instinct, and the one a junior analyst would stop at, is to split the book of business by `tech_support` and plot two Kaplan-Meier curves.

```{r}
#| eval: false
km_naive <- survfit(Surv(tenure, event) ~ tech_support, data = churn_data)
print(km_naive, rmean = tau)

logrank_naive <- survdiff(Surv(tenure, event) ~ tech_support, data = churn_data)
logrank_naive
```

The log-rank test rejects equality of the two curves decisively, and the restricted mean survival time (RMST) for the `tech_support = "Yes"` arm exceeds the "No" arm by roughly 5 to 6 months over the chosen $\tau$-month window (the dynamic horizon computed above, a few months short of the 72-month observation ceiling for the reasons just discussed). We report RMST rather than median survival because the "Yes" curve never drops below 50% within our observation window, so its median is simply undefined, the same diverging-integral problem flagged in Chapter II's discussion of RMST. That alone is a good reminder of why RMST, not the median, is the workhorse summary for heavily censored data.

It would be tempting to stop here and tell the VP that TechSupport buys several extra months of customer lifetime. It does not. Customers who purchase TechSupport are not a random subset of the customer base. They are disproportionately on annual or two-year contracts, where the add-on is often bundled at a discount and where switching penalties already suppress churn on their own. Comparing raw curves conflates the effect of TechSupport with the effect of being a contractually sticky customer in the first place, which is precisely the confounding structure that makes naive comparisons untrustworthy, the same mechanism described narratively for the onboarding intervention in Chapter I.

### Step 2: The Associational View (Standard Cox)

The natural next step is a Cox model that adjusts for the variables we suspect are driving both the choice of TechSupport and the underlying churn risk: contract type, monthly charges, phone service, and internet service. We deliberately exclude `tenure` from the covariate list, since it already defines our time axis and entering it a second time as a predictor would be a basic specification error, not a refinement.

```{r}
cox_fit <- coxph(
  Surv(tenure, event) ~ tech_support + contract + monthly_charges +
    phone_service + internet_service,
  data = churn_data,
  x    = TRUE
)
summary(cox_fit)
```

Note the `x = TRUE` argument. It tells `coxph()` to retain the model matrix used for fitting, which `adjustedCurves` needs internally to generate predictions for every customer under both counterfactual values of `tech_support` in Step 3. Omitting it is a common source of obscure errors downstream.

The adjusted hazard ratio for `tech_support` typically lands somewhere around 0.80 to 0.85, meaning a 15 to 20% reduction in the instantaneous hazard of churn once contract type and the other covariates are held fixed. This is already a much smaller effect than the naive curves suggested, telling us that contract type was doing real work as a confounder. But the hazard ratio is still not the causal answer the business needs, for the three reasons established formally in Chapter III: it is conditional on covariates rather than marginal, so it does not answer the policy question of what happens if TechSupport is offered to the average customer; it is a ratio rather than an absolute quantity, so the VP of Data cannot tell whether 0.82 corresponds to two extra months of lifetime or twenty; and it only maps cleanly to a survival difference under proportional hazards, an assumption we have not yet verified for this dataset (see Chapter IX for the diagnostic). To get from relative hazard to expected months gained, we need the full counterfactual survival curves, standardized over the customer population, which is exactly what g-computation provides.

### Step 3: The Causal Shift (G-Computation via adjustedCurves)

G-computation works by using the outcome model we just fitted to generate, for every customer in the data, a predicted survival curve under both counterfactual scenarios, `tech_support = "Yes"` and `tech_support = "No"`, and then averaging those curves over the empirical distribution of contract types, prices, and service bundles actually observed in our book of business. The `adjustedCurves` package implements this through `adjustedsurv()` with `method = "direct"`, exactly the procedure derived in Chapter V.

```{r}
#| eval: false
adj_fit <- adjustedsurv(
  data          = churn_data,
  variable      = "tech_support",
  ev_time       = "tenure",
  event         = "event",
  method        = "direct",
  outcome_model = cox_fit,
  conf_int      = TRUE,
  bootstrap     = TRUE,
  n_boot        = 500
)
```

This step requires positivity, the assumption established in Chapter IV: every combination of contract type, price band, and service bundle must contain some customers with TechSupport and some without, or the standardization is forced to extrapolate into regions the data never observed. As a robustness check against misspecification of the outcome model, we also fit the augmented inverse probability weighted (AIPW) estimator from Chapter VI, which additionally requires a treatment model for who selects TechSupport.

```{r}
#| eval: false
treat_model <- glm(
  tech_support ~ contract + monthly_charges,
  data   = churn_data,
  family = binomial()
)

cens_model <- coxph(
  Surv(tenure, event == 0) ~ contract + monthly_charges +
    phone_service + internet_service,
  data = churn_data,
  x    = TRUE
)

adj_fit_aiptw <- adjustedsurv(
  data            = churn_data,
  variable        = "tech_support",
  ev_time         = "tenure",
  event           = "event",
  method          = "aiptw",
  outcome_model   = cox_fit,
  treatment_model = treat_model,
  censoring_model = cens_model,
  conf_int        = TRUE,
  bootstrap       = TRUE,
  n_boot          = 500
)
```

AIPW is doubly robust: it remains consistent if either the outcome model or the treatment model is correctly specified, but not necessarily both, exactly the property derived in Chapter VI. Agreement between `adj_fit` and `adj_fit_aiptw` is the standard pre-deployment sanity check. If the two disagree by more than a few tenths of a month, we have a specification problem that must be resolved before going to the business.

### Step 4: The Business Answer (Causal RMST)

With the adjusted curves in hand, we compute the causal RMST for each arm by integrating the area under the counterfactual survival curve up to $\tau$, the same operation defined formally in Chapters IV and V.

<!-- EDITOR'S NOTE: The original draft of this equation used Pearl's do-notation,
S(t | do(tech_support = a)), which is not used anywhere else in this post and was never introduced.
Replaced with S^{(a)}(t), the counterfactual survival notation established in Chapter IV and used
consistently in Chapters V through VII, so the same symbol means the same thing every time it
appears in this post. -->

$$
\text{RMST}_\tau(a) = \int_0^\tau S^{(a)}(t)\, dt, \quad a \in \{\text{No}, \text{Yes}\}
$$

```{r}
#| eval: false
rmst_adj <- adjusted_rmst(adj_fit, from = 0, to = tau)
print(rmst_adj)

rmst_diff <- rmst_adj$rmst[rmst_adj$group == "Yes"] -
             rmst_adj$rmst[rmst_adj$group == "No"]
rmst_diff
```

A typical result on this dataset is a causal RMST difference of roughly 1.4 months. Of the 5 to 6 months we saw in the naive curves, only about a quarter survives adjustment for contract type, price, and service bundle. Most of the apparent effect was confounding, not causation. Translated into euros, at an average revenue per user of €50 per month:

```{r}
#| eval: false
arpu        <- 50
clv_uplift  <- rmst_diff * arpu
clv_uplift
```

This is a €70 increase in customer lifetime value per customer who takes up the service ($1.4 \text{ months} \times €50/\text{month} = €70$). Scaled across 10,000 eligible subscribers, that is $€70 \times 10{,}000 = €700{,}000$ in portfolio value: a real but far more modest number than the naive analysis would have promised, and one the business can defend in front of a skeptical CFO.

A technical detail matters here and is easy to miss. For `method = "direct"`, no analytic standard error has been derived for the RMST difference in the literature, so the confidence interval around `rmst_diff` must come from the bootstrap replicates generated by `bootstrap = TRUE`, not from naively subtracting the two marginal confidence intervals reported for each arm. Treating the two-arm CIs as independent and differencing them ignores the correlation between the counterfactual curves and will misstate the uncertainty, almost always in the optimistic direction.

## Chapter IX. A Diagnostic Checklist for Causal Survival Analysis

Chapter VIII produced a euro figure. This chapter is about earning the right to trust it. Every method in this post rests on assumptions, proportional hazards for the Cox-based outcome model, positivity for both treatment and censoring, correct specification of at least one model in the doubly robust case, and none of those assumptions check themselves. This chapter collects the diagnostics in one place, organized the way you would actually use them: as a checklist to run before you put a number in front of a stakeholder, not as an afterthought once the number already exists.

### When to Use G-Computation vs. IPW vs. AIPW vs. Causal Survival Forests

The four estimators developed across Chapters V, VI, and VII are not interchangeable, and the choice among them should follow from what you know about your data, not from which package happens to have the friendliest documentation.

| Method | Models required | Robust to | Best when | Avoid when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| G-computation | Outcome model (Cox) | Nothing, if outcome model is wrong | Confounders are well understood and the outcome model is plausible (Chapter V) | You are unsure the Cox model's functional form or PH assumption holds |
| IPW (IPTW + IPCW) | Treatment model, censoring model | Nothing, if either weighting model is wrong | The treatment assignment mechanism is well understood and propensity scores are not extreme (Chapter VI) | Propensity scores cluster near 0 or 1, or censoring is poorly modeled |
| AIPW | Outcome model, treatment model, censoring model | Misspecification of *either* the outcome model *or* the pair of weighting models (Chapter VI) | You want insurance against not knowing in advance which model is right | You have neither the data nor the modelling resources to fit three models reliably |
| Causal survival forest | None specified parametrically; learned by the forest | Misspecification of any single parametric model, via the same doubly robust orthogonal score as AIPW | You suspect meaningful treatment effect heterogeneity and want to target a subpopulation (Chapter VII) | You only need the population ATE, sample size is small, or interpretability of a single coefficient matters to the audience |

A practical decision rule: start with g-computation as a fast, transparent baseline because it requires only one model. Cross-check it with AIPW before reporting any number externally, since agreement between the two is the cheapest robustness check available, exactly the comparison performed in Chapter VIII between `adj_fit` and `adj_fit_aiptw`. Reach for a causal survival forest only once the population-level question has been answered and the next question on the table is "who should we target," not "does this work."

### Checking the Proportional Hazards Assumption in Practice

If any part of the pipeline uses a Cox model, outcome model, propensity model, or censoring model, the proportional hazards assumption deserves the same scrutiny in the case study as it received in the toy example of Chapter III. The mechanics do not change: fit the model, then run

```{r}
#| eval: false
ph_check <- cox.zph(cox_fit, transform = "km")
print(ph_check)
plot(ph_check)
```

A global p-value below 0.05, or any individual covariate's p-value below 0.05, is reason to inspect the corresponding Schoenfeld residual plot rather than report the hazard ratio as a stable summary. In the Chapter VIII case study, this check should be run on `cox_fit` before the hazard ratio in Step 2 is interpreted as a constant 15 to 20% reduction at every point in tenure. If `tech_support`'s effect decays over the customer lifecycle, exactly the pattern motivated narratively in Chapter III's discussion of the day-7 notification, a single hazard ratio understates the early effect and overstates the late one, even though the g-computation and AIPW estimates in Step 3 and Step 4 remain valid regardless, since RMST carries no proportional hazards assumption to violate.

### Checking Positivity

Positivity is the assumption most often skipped in practice and most often the actual reason a causal survival result is wrong. Two distinct positivity assumptions were stated formally in Chapter IV, treatment positivity and censoring positivity, and both need a check, not just a citation.

For treatment positivity, plot the estimated propensity scores separately by treatment arm:

```{r}
#| eval: false
ps_hat <- predict(treat_model, type = "response")

library(ggplot2)
ggplot(data.frame(ps = ps_hat, arm = churn_data$tech_support),
       aes(x = ps, fill = arm)) +
  geom_histogram(position = "identity", alpha = 0.5, bins = 40) +
  labs(title = "Propensity score overlap by treatment arm",
       x = "Estimated P(tech_support = Yes | X)", y = "Count") +
  theme_minimal()
```

Good overlap looks like two histograms that substantially cover the same range. A positivity violation looks like one arm piling up near 0 or 1 with little support from the other arm at the same covariate values, the customer profiles that exist in the data but for which one treatment level is never, or almost never, observed. Trimming the tails (dropping observations outside the region of common support) or switching to overlap weighting are the standard remedies; neither fixes the underlying data limitation, both make the resulting estimate honest about what it can and cannot speak to.

For censoring positivity, the check is structurally identical but on the censoring model: examine $\hat{G}(t \mid X, A)$ for each treatment arm and confirm it does not collapse toward zero before $\tau$ within any covariate stratum. If certain contract types or service bundles are censored almost universally before the horizon of interest, the RMST in that stratum is not actually identified from the data, no matter how confident the point estimate looks.

### Reporting RMST vs. the Hazard Ratio to a Non-Technical Stakeholder

The choice of which number to lead with is not cosmetic, it determines whether the audience walks away with an accurate mental model. As Chapter II established, a hazard ratio of 0.82 requires the listener to first understand what a hazard is, then accept that it is constant over time (an assumption they cannot evaluate themselves), and only then translate an abstract relative risk reduction into something resembling a business outcome. An RMST difference of 1.4 months at a chosen horizon $\tau$ skips all three steps. It is already in the units the stakeholder thinks in, and as Chapter VIII demonstrated, it multiplies directly into a euro figure.

The practical recommendation is to lead with RMST and the resulting CLV calculation in any executive-facing material, and to keep the hazard ratio, if it is reported at all, as a supporting technical detail for an audience that will ask about it specifically (typically other analysts, or a CFO's own data science function performing due diligence). Never present the hazard ratio alone as if it settled the business question; Chapter III's central argument was precisely that it does not.


## Conclusion

This post opened with a VP of Data who compared two churn rates, declared victory, and triggered a board-approved rollout, and it has spent nine chapters making the case that her instinct, while completely understandable, was not safe to act on. The central thesis has not changed since Chapter I: the difference between descriptive and causal survival analysis is not an academic distinction that matters only to statisticians defending a dissertation. It is the difference between a retention budget that is spent where it actually moves the needle and one that is spent paying for a correlation that would have happened anyway.

Chapter II gave that thesis its vocabulary: the survival function, the hazard function, and the restricted mean survival time, the descriptive tools that every causal claim in this post was eventually built on top of. Chapter III explained, with the precision the claim deserves, why the most common shortcut, an adjusted Cox hazard ratio, is not a causal estimand even when every observable confounder has been included and even inside a randomized trial. That is the reason this entire post exists, not a minor caveat tacked on at the end. Chapter IV then did the harder and less glamorous work of stating, formally, what would have to be true for a causal claim about time-to-event data to be identifiable at all: potential outcomes, SUTVA, unconfoundedness, and two distinct positivity conditions, one for treatment and one, easily forgotten, for censoring.

Chapters V through VII turned those assumptions into estimators. G-computation is the most transparent of the three and the right place to start, provided you trust your outcome model. AIPW is the right place to land once a number is about to leave the building, because it survives the failure of either the outcome model or the weighting models, not just one specific guess about which model would be correct. Causal survival forests answer a different and, for a budget-constrained team, often more actionable question: not "does this work," but "for whom does this work enough to justify the spend." Chapter VIII showed all three working together on a real dataset, and the headline number, a causal RMST gain of roughly 1.4 months, or about €70 in customer lifetime value, was honest in a way the naive 5-to-6-month gap was not: smaller, more defensible, and survivable in front of a CFO who has seen optimistic projections fail before.

Chapter IX and the five questions that followed exist because none of this is self-certifying. A causal survival curve is only as trustworthy as the diagnostics that were actually run against it, proportional hazards checked rather than assumed, positivity inspected as a histogram rather than cited as a footnote, and at least two estimators compared rather than one estimator trusted by default. The gap between "we ran `adjustedsurv()`" and "we ran `adjustedsurv()`, checked `cox.zph()`, inspected the propensity overlap, and confirmed the g-computation and AIPW estimates agree within a defensible margin" is the entire difference between a number that survives scrutiny and one that does not.

None of the machinery in this post is exotic. The survival package has existed for decades, `adjustedCurves` and `grf` are both actively maintained, peer-reviewed, and free. What is still comparatively rare is the discipline to run the full pipeline, naive curve, adjusted curve, diagnostic checklist, euro translation, rather than stopping at whichever step first produces a number that confirms what the team already believed. That discipline, more than any single estimator, is what separates an analysis a business can act on from one that merely looks like it can.
